# ^ Preface

This is the curriculum for grades 4–6. It is for children who can read a rule, keep a score, and argue about whose turn it is.

The band has one job: **the learner can write a rule, test it, search a space, and say what has not been tried.** The three years build that job from a single conditional to a working rule base with a visible boundary.

The document is standalone. It contains the year map, eighteen unit cards, two scripted lessons, the assessment, the common errors and the out-of-bounds list.

Three principles shape the band.

**A rule is a claim, not a command.** *If it rains, we stay inside* can be true or false. The child learns to test the rule, not just to obey it.

**Search is a physical act before it is a mental one.** Children walk a search space with cards, then draw it, then read it. The drawing comes before the algorithm.

**The first screen is a window, not an oracle.** The machine shows what follows from the facts it has. It does not show what is missing. The child learns to ask both questions in the same lesson.

# ^ How to use this document

## ^ The shape of the year

One 45-minute lesson each week, for 36 weeks. Three years, 108 lessons. The lessons sit inside mathematics, science, English or a weekly thinking slot. They do not require a new subject.

Each unit is six lessons. The sixth lesson is the explanation: the learner shows the artefact to someone outside the class and answers three questions — *what does it say, what does it not say, and how would you check?*

The last unit of year 3 is the first supervised screen lesson. It is not a programming course. It is one query, one prediction, one missing fact.

## ^ The shape of a unit card

**Question.** The one question the unit teaches the learner to ask.

**Do.** Three activities that make the question operational.

**Words.** The vocabulary the learner should be able to use by the end.

**Evidence.** What the teacher keeps, and what it shows.

**Out of bounds.** What not to introduce in this unit, even if a learner asks.

## ^ The five-move lesson

The lesson shape is the same as grades 1–3: a thing in the hand, a question with a gap, a try, a compare, a name. The materials change. The rule does not.

In this band the "thing in the hand" is often a rule card, a search board or a source. The "name" is often a distinction the class has already made three times — *needed* versus *enough*, *tried* versus *all*, *said* versus *meant*.

## ^ What the learner can do at the end

A learner who completes the band can:

- write a conditional rule and test it against a case;
- distinguish a condition that is needed from a condition that is enough;
- use *all*, *some* and *none* without treating *some* as *all*;
- draw a small search space and say whether a search was complete;
- explain a recursive rule — the nest and the floor — with a family tree;
- find a counterexample to a general claim;
- state a hidden assumption in a rule or a headline;
- audit three sources on one claim and say what each leaves out;
- run one query in a supervised natural-language rule system, predict the answer, and name one fact the system does not have.

## ^ What the band does not do

It does not teach truth tables. It does not teach Prolog syntax before age ten, and only as a reading exercise after that. It does not use "valid" as a synonym for "true". It does not name fallacies. It does not teach probability. Those belong to later bands.

# ^ The four questions

The four classroom rules from grades 1–3 become four questions. The change is deliberate: a rule tells the child what to do; a question tells her what to ask when no one is telling her.

| The rule | The question | The maxim behind it |
|---|---|---|
| Say true things. | Is it true? | Quality |
| Say enough. | Is it enough? | Quantity |
| Say what fits. | Does it fit? | Relation |
| Say it clearly. | Is it clear? | Manner |

## ^ Is it true?

The learner asks for the source. *I saw it*, *someone told me*, *I read it*, *I guessed*. She learns that a source can be honest and wrong, and that a confident source is not thereby a good one.

**Practice.** A headline, a photograph and a number. Which part is observed? Which part is inferred? Which part is missing?

## ^ Is it enough?

The learner asks whether the information lets her act. This is the band's central question, and it has two forms.

**Enough information.** A recipe that says "add the flour" but not how much is true and not enough.

**Enough condition.** A key is needed to open the door, but the right key is not enough if the door is bolted. The learner meets this distinction in unit 1.2 and uses it for three years.

## ^ Does it fit?

The learner asks whether the answer addresses the question. A true statement can be irrelevant. A relevant statement can be true and useless. The test is the question, not the interest of the answer.

## ^ Is it clear?

The learner asks whether a listener could act on the message. She learns three tools: define the term, give the order, remove the ambiguity.

**The ambiguity game.** *Visiting relatives can be boring.* Who is visiting? The learner draws both readings. The drawing is the proof that the sentence had two meanings.

# ^ Saying and meaning

At this age the learner can meet the gap between what is said and what is meant without the technical vocabulary. Three patterns are enough.

## ^ Some does not mean all

> **Teacher:** Some children have finished the task.

The learner infers that not all have finished. Nothing in the words says so. The inference comes from the assumption that the speaker would have said "all" if all were true.

**Practice.** A tray of ten cups, six with a sticker. The teacher says, "Some cups have stickers." True. Does it mean all? No. Does it mean only six? Not exactly. The word means *at least one*. In ordinary talk it usually implicates *not all*, because a cooperative speaker would say *all* if all were true. The learner sorts the sentences that are *said*, *meant* and *both*.

## ^ Or can exclude both

> **Teacher:** You can have the apple or the pear.

The learner often hears: not both. The words do not say it. The inference comes from the assumption that the speaker would have mentioned both if both were allowed.

**Practice.** A menu with two options. The teacher says "or" and then allows both. The learner discovers that the word underdetermines the permission. The rule of the game is not "or always excludes"; it is "the listener infers an exclusion unless something cancels it".

## ^ If is not only if

> **Rule:** If you finish your work, you may go outside.

The learner often hears: if and only if. The rule does not say that finishing is the only way out. It says finishing is enough.

**Practice.** Three cases: finished and went out; did not finish and went out (the rule is silent); finished and did not go out (the rule is broken). The third case is the test. The second is the confusion.

## ^ What did they leave out?

A headline says: *School scores rise after new timetable.* The learner asks: compared with what? which school? which scores? did anything else change?

This is the ghost rule in its first form: **write down what they did not say, then judge it.** The phrase belongs to the band. The learner uses it on headlines, advertisements and her own explanations.

Do not use the word *implicature* in year 1 or year 2. In year 3, at the end, the teacher may say: "The name for what we have been doing is implicature. You will meet it again." The name is a handle, not a new lesson.

# 1. Year 1 — Rules and conditions

## ^ The year's question

What follows from a rule, and what does it need?

The first year turns the classroom rule into an object of study. The learner writes rules, tests them, breaks them, and repairs them. She meets the two distinctions that carry the rest of the band: *needed* versus *enough*, and *all* versus *some*.

## From rule to conditional

**Question.** What shape does a rule have?

**Do.** Collect classroom rules. Rewrite each as *if ... then ...* or *... unless ...*. Test one rule on three cases: the condition holds and the result holds; the condition fails and the result does not; the condition fails and the result still holds. Which case breaks the rule? Only the first and third matter; the second is silence.

**Words.** if, then, unless, condition, result, rule, case, holds.

**Evidence.** The learner rewrites three rules and identifies the case that would break one.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use arrows or truth tables. Do not use "antecedent" or "consequent".

## Needed and enough

**Question.** Is this condition needed, or is it enough?

**Do.** The door and the key. The trip and the signed slip. The cake and the flour. For each, sort the conditions into *needed*, *enough*, *both*, *neither*. Then find a case where a needed condition is present and the result still fails. That case is the lesson.

**Words.** needed, enough, both, neither, condition, missing, add.

**Evidence.** The learner gives one condition that is needed but not enough, and one that is both.

**Out of bounds.** Do not introduce the Latin terms. *Necessary* and *sufficient* are acceptable in year 2; the experience comes first.

## All, some, none

**Question.** How much is *some*?

**Do.** A set of ten objects. Make true sentences with *all*, *some*, *none*. Swap a sentence with a partner and find a counterexample. Then the tray of cups: *some have stickers*. Is *all* true? Is *none* true? What exactly does *some* rule out?

**Words.** all, every, some, none, at least one, not all, counterexample.

**Evidence.** The learner writes a *some* sentence and says what it does not mean.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use the symbols ∀ and ∃. Do not teach the square of opposition.

## The rule with a hole

**Question.** What does a rule with a blank in it say?

**Do.** *If a ___ is on the list, the ___ may go.* Fill the blanks with cards. One rule, many cases. Then reverse: given three cases, what is the rule? The blank is a variable: a place where many things can go, one at a time.

**Words.** blank, hole, variable, fill, case, same rule, different case.

**Evidence.** The learner writes one rule with two holes and applies it to three cases.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use `X` or `Y` as notation. The blank is a blank.

## Testing a rule

**Question.** How do we try to break a rule?

**Do.** The learner writes a rule. The partner's job is to find a case where the condition holds and the result fails. If the partner finds one, the rule is either wrong or incomplete. Choose: repair the rule or add an exception. Record the choice in the rule log.

**Words.** test, break, counterexample, repair, exception, log.

**Evidence.** The learner records one attempted refutation and one repair.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use "falsify" as a technical term. Do not name the fallacy of the converse; show the case.

## The class rule base

**Question.** Can a set of rules be checked together?

**Do.** The class writes five rules for a fictional school trip. Each rule has a name, a condition and a result. Add one exception and one *we don't know* card. Test the base with five cases. Which rules fire together? Which conflict? Which case has no rule at all?

**Words.** rule base, fact, rule, query, exception, conflict, no rule.

**Evidence.** The five-rule base, with one exception and one unknown, explained by the learner.

**Out of bounds.** Do not put the base on a screen yet. The paper version must be secure.

# 2. Year 2 — Search and recursion

## ^ The year's question

How do we search, and when are we done?

The second year makes search visible. The learner walks a space, draws it, and logs it. She meets the difference between *I found an answer* and *I found all the answers*. She meets recursion as a nest with a floor, not as a trick.

## The search space

**Question.** What are we looking through?

**Do.** A map with six rooms and two doors each. The learner lists the possible routes before trying any. Draw the routes as a tree on the floor with tape. Count the branches. The drawing is the search space; the walking is the search.

**Words.** search, space, route, branch, choice, tree, start, end.

**Evidence.** The learner draws a search tree for a six-room map and counts the routes.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use "node" or "graph" as formal terms. Tree and branch are enough.

## Try, fail, go back

**Question.** What do we do when a choice fails?

**Do.** A locked box with three keys. Try one, fail, put it back, try the next. The learner logs each try: *tried*, *result*, *next*. Then a route-finding game where one branch dead-ends. The learner must return to the last choice and take the other branch.

**Words.** try, fail, go back, back up, last choice, next, log.

**Evidence.** The learner completes a search log with one backtrack and names the choice point.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use "backtracking" as a term until the end of the unit; then name it once.

## Have we tried everything?

**Question.** How do we know the search is complete?

**Do.** Two searches: one stops at the first answer; one continues to the end. Compare the results. Ask: which question needs all the answers? Which needs only one? Count the branches and check them off. A search is complete when every branch has a mark.

**Words.** complete, all, every, branch, check off, stop early, first answer.

**Evidence.** The learner says whether a search was complete, and shows the marks that justify the claim.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use "exhaustive" as a synonym for "tired". Use *complete*.

## The nest

**Question.** What is a rule that contains itself?

**Do.** A family tree. *Ana's mother is Bea. Bea's mother is Cleo.* Ask: who is Ana's grandmother? Then: *An ancestor of Ana is either her mother or an ancestor of her mother.* Walk the nest with cards. Stop when there is no card left.

**Words.** nest, ancestor, mother, inside, again, stop, chain.

**Evidence.** The learner uses the ancestor rule to answer a two-step question and shows the chain.

**Out of bounds.** Do not write a recursive program. Do not use "recursion" as a term until the end of the unit; then name it once.

## The floor of the nest

**Question.** How does a recursive rule stop?

**Do.** Remove the floor from the ancestor rule. What happens? The learner walks the nest with no base case and loops. Then add the floor: *Ana's mother is Bea* is a fact, not a rule. The fact stops the chain. Every nest needs a floor.

**Words.** floor, base, stop, fact, rule, loop, again and again.

**Evidence.** The learner explains why the rule without a fact never stops.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use "base case" as a term before the learner has walked the loop. Then name it.

## The search log and the debug log

**Question.** What does a good record of a search contain?

**Do.** Design a two-page log. Page one: the search — start, choices, tries, result, complete? Page two: the debug — prediction, what happened, why, repair. Use the log on a new puzzle. Swap logs with a partner and follow the partner's log without speaking.

**Words.** log, prediction, result, why, repair, complete, record.

**Evidence.** A log that a partner can follow to the same result.

**Out of bounds.** Do not require a standard form. The learner's log is the artefact; the teacher's template is a scaffold, not a cage.

# 3. Year 3 — Sources, consistency and the edge

## ^ The year's question

What is missing, and how would we know?

The third year turns from the rule to its warrant. Where did the fact come from? What would make the rule base inconsistent? What is not on the list, and what does that mean? The year ends with the first supervised screen lesson, where the machine shows its boundary.

## Two facts that cannot both be true

**Question.** What happens when the facts conflict?

**Do.** A fact wall with a contradiction: *The trip is on Tuesday* and *The trip is on Thursday*. The learner finds it, marks it, and chooses a repair: remove one, date one, or ask. Then a rule base where two rules give different answers for the same case. Which rule wins? Why?

**Words.** conflict, contradict, both cannot be true, repair, remove, date, ask, priority.

**Evidence.** The learner finds a contradiction and states the repair she chose.

**Out of bounds.** Do not teach paraconsistent logic. Do not let "both are true" stand as a repair.

## The counterexample

**Question.** How many cases does it take to break a general claim?

**Do.** General claims: *All birds can fly. All metals are hard. Everyone who smiles is happy.* The learner hunts for one case. One is enough. The penguin, the mercury, the polite unhappy person. The claim can be repaired with *most*, *usually* or *except*.

**Words.** general, claim, counterexample, one case, enough, except, usually.

**Evidence.** The learner produces one counterexample and one repaired claim.

**Out of bounds.** Do not name the fallacies. The counterexample is the tool.

## The ghost rule

**Question.** What did they not say?

**Do.** A short policy: *Learners may use the library at break if they have a pass.* The learner writes the unstated conditions: a pass from whom? which library? which break? what counts as use? Then a headline: *New rule improves behaviour.* What is the ghost? Compared with what? Which behaviour? Over what time?

**Words.** ghost, unstated, assumption, missing, compared with, who says, what counts.

**Evidence.** The learner writes two ghost rules for one policy and says which one changes the conclusion.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use "implicit premise" as a term until grades 7–9. Ghost is the band's word.

## Where the fact came from

**Question.** Which source should I trust, and for what?

**Do.** One claim, three sources: a primary document, a news report of it, and a social-media post. The learner fills a source grid: *who*, *how they know*, *what they leave out*, *what would change my mind*. Then the expert case: the dentist on teeth, the dentist on taxes.

**Words.** source, primary, report, post, expert, domain, leave out, check.

**Evidence.** A source grid with three rows and one named omission per row.

**Out of bounds.** Do not teach "lateral reading" as a label; do it. Open a second tab, check the claim elsewhere.

## Not on the list is not false

**Question.** What does *no* mean in a list?

**Do.** A class list of allowed equipment. A learner asks: are goggles allowed? They are not on the list. Does that mean forbidden, or not yet decided? The learner distinguishes *not on the list*, *forbidden*, and *not known*. Then a library catalogue: the book is not in the catalogue. Does that mean it does not exist?

**Words.** on the list, not on the list, forbidden, not known, closed, open, check.

**Evidence.** The learner sorts three cases into *forbidden*, *not listed*, *not known* and says what to do next.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use "closed-world assumption" as a term until grade 9. The distinction is the lesson.

## First screen: the machine's boundary

**Question.** What does the machine know, and what does it not know?

**Do.** The teacher projects one natural-language rule base, for example a school-trip rule: *A learner may go on the trip if the learner has a signed slip and the learner is on the list.* The class predicts the answer for three learners before the query runs. Then run it. Then add one fact and run again. Finally, write the boundary note: one fact the system does not have, and one question it cannot answer.

**Words.** program, fact, rule, query, run, answer, missing, boundary, not in the program.

**Evidence.** The prediction sheet, the run, and the boundary note. This is the first screen artefact.

**Out of bounds.** Do not teach syntax. Do not let the learner type a program from scratch. Do not treat the output as true. The output is *what follows from the facts entered*, not *what is true in the world*.

## The screen protocol

Use the same six steps every time.

1. **Read the rules aloud.** The class says what each rule needs.
2. **Predict.** Each learner writes the answer for three cases before anything runs.
3. **Run one query.** The teacher runs it; the class watches.
4. **Compare.** Which prediction was right? Which fact made the difference?
5. **Add one fact.** Run again. What changed? What did not?
6. **Write the boundary.** One fact the system does not have; one question it cannot answer.

The protocol is the assessment. A learner who can predict and then explain a surprise has the concept. A learner who can only read the output has a demonstration.

# 4. A scripted lesson: needed, but not enough

**Year 1, unit 1.2. Time: 45 minutes. Group: 24 learners.**

**Question.** Is a signed slip enough to go on the trip?

**Materials.** A large sheet divided into four boxes: *needed*, *enough*, *both*, *neither*. Eight condition cards: signed slip, on the list, bus seat, packed lunch, wearing uniform, parent at home, good weather, finished homework. A story card with three learners.

## ^ Minute 0–5: the slip

> **Teacher:** The trip is on Friday. I have this signed slip. Can I go?

The class says yes. The teacher puts the slip in the *needed* box.

> **Teacher:** Is the slip enough?

Some say yes. The teacher tells the story of a learner with a signed slip who is not on the list. The bus leaves without her.

> **Teacher:** What did she need that she did not have?

The class adds *on the list* to *needed*. The teacher moves both cards to *both* and writes: *signed slip + on the list*.

## ^ Minute 5–15: the sort

Pairs sort the eight cards into the four boxes. The teacher circulates and asks one question per pair:

> What would you add to this card to make it enough?

The question exposes the difference between a condition and a set of conditions. A pair that says "nothing" for *signed slip* has not met the story. A pair that says "on the list" has.

## ^ Minute 15–25: the three learners

The story card has three learners.

- **Ana:** signed slip, on the list, bus seat.
- **Ben:** signed slip, not on the list, bus seat.
- **Cara:** no slip, on the list, bus seat.

The class decides who may go. Ana yes. Ben no. Cara no. Then the teacher changes one fact: the bus is full. Who may go now? Ana no. The rule was true and the world changed. A rule can be satisfied and the action still blocked.

## ^ Minute 25–35: the rule

The class writes the rule:

> If a learner has a signed slip and is on the list and there is a seat on the bus, the learner may go on the trip.

Then the teacher asks: **Is this rule true?** Yes. **Is it complete?** The class looks for a case it does not cover. A learner with a slip, on the list, a seat, but the trip is cancelled. The rule is silent. Add the condition or accept the silence. The choice must be stated.

## ^ Minute 35–42: the ghost

The teacher shows a notice:

> *All learners with a signed slip may go on the trip.*

The class writes what the notice does not say. On the list. A seat. The date. Who signed. The class chooses the ghost that matters most. The choice is the beginning of unit 3.3.

## ^ Minute 42–45: exit ticket

> Write one condition that is needed but not enough. Write one condition that is enough.

The teacher sorts the tickets into three piles: *condition only*, *set of conditions*, *confused*. The piles set the next lesson.

## ^ What the teacher watches for

- A learner who says "the slip is enough" has the *needed* half. Ask: what else did Ana need?
- A learner who says "nothing is enough" has seen too many exceptions. Ask: what would be enough for this trip, today?
- A learner who says "it depends" is ready for the rule. Ask: depends on which fact?

## ^ What not to do

Do not introduce the words *necessary* and *sufficient* until the class has sorted three examples. Then name the distinction once and use it. The label is a compression, not a lesson.

# 5. The first screen lesson

**Year 3, unit 3.6. Time: 20 minutes inside a 45-minute lesson.**

The purpose is not to teach programming. It is to make the boundary visible.

## Before the screen

The class has already built the paper rule base. The teacher projects the same rules in a natural-language system. The class reads them aloud and checks that they match the paper. If they do not match, the class fixes the paper first. The machine is not allowed to introduce a rule the class has not already written.

## The prediction sheet

| Learner | Signed slip | On the list | Seat | Prediction | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ana | yes | yes | yes | may go | |
| Ben | yes | no | yes | may not go | |
| Cara | no | yes | yes | may not go | |

Every learner fills the sheet before the first run. The prediction is the assessment, not the result.

## The run

The teacher runs one query at a time. The class compares. If a result surprises the class, the teacher asks:

> Which fact made the difference? Point to it.

The learner must point to a fact in the program, not to the world. This is the first lesson in the difference between *what the program says* and *what is true*.

## The boundary note

Each learner writes two sentences.

> The program does not know that ___.
>
> It cannot answer the question ___.

A good boundary note names a fact the program lacks (the bus is full) and a question the program cannot answer (should the trip be cancelled?). A weak note says "it does not know everything". The weak note is corrected by asking: which thing?

## After the screen

The class returns to paper. They add the missing fact to the paper rule base, then decide whether to add it to the program. The decision is recorded in the debug log. The machine has become a participant in the paper argument, not a replacement for it.

# 6. Assessment

## The three artefacts

**The rule base.** Five rules, one exception, one *we don't know* card. Each rule has a name and a source. The learner explains it aloud and answers: What is on the wall? What follows? What do we ask? What is not on the wall?

**The debug log.** Two pages. A prediction, a result, a reason and a repair. The log must show one case where the learner was wrong. A log with no wrong prediction is a log that was written after the fact.

**The source audit.** One claim, three sources. For each source: who says it, how they know, what they leave out. Then one sentence: *I would change my mind if ___.*

## The gate descriptors

| Gate | The learner can... | The evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | write a conditional and test it on a case | a rule card and a case card that breaks it |
| 4 | distinguish needed from enough | one condition of each kind, with an example |
| 5 | draw a search space and say whether a search was complete | a tree with every branch marked |
| 5 | explain a recursive rule and its floor | the ancestor chain, with the stopping fact |
| 6 (seed) | find a counterexample to a general claim | one case and one repaired claim |
| 6 (seed) | state a hidden assumption in a policy | two ghost rules, one that matters |
| 6 (seed) | run one query, predict the answer, and name a missing fact | the prediction sheet and the boundary note |

## Moderation

Two teachers, one artefact, blind. Each writes one sentence: what the learner can do, and what she cannot yet do. If the sentences disagree, the task is ambiguous. Fix the task.

## What not to assess

Do not assess the number of rules. A base of five good rules is better than a base of twenty copied ones. Do not assess the speed of the search. Do not assess whether the learner uses the word *backtracking*. Assess whether she can go back.

# 7. Common errors, and what to do

| What the learner says | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| "If it's a rule, it always applies." | No experience of exceptions | Give the rule a case it did not expect; ask what to change |
| "A signed slip is enough." | Needed confused with enough | The trip story; ask what else Ana needed |
| "Some means all." | Scalar inference ignored | The tray of cups; ask what *some* rules out |
| "If P then Q, so if Q then P." | The converse, unnamed | Find a case where Q is true and P is false; do not name the fallacy yet |
| "It's not on the list, so it's forbidden." | Closed world treated as open question | Sort *forbidden*, *not listed*, *not known* |
| "The computer says yes." | Output treated as truth | Ask: which facts did you enter? what is missing? |
| "I found one answer, so I'm done." | Search stopped early | Ask: is the question *find one* or *find all*? |
| "I searched everywhere." | No record of the search | Point to the marks on the branches |

## The one error to pre-empt

**Needed is not enough.** It is the error that survives into adulthood, where it appears as *we have the policy, so we are safe*, *we have the test, so we know*, *we have the key, so the door opens*. The correction is a question, not a definition: **What else would have to be true?**

# 8. Out of bounds

| Not this | Why not yet | Where it enters |
|---|---|---|
| Truth tables | The table would replace the case | Grades 10–12 |
| Prolog syntax before age ten | The symbols would replace the rule | Grade 9, as reading |
| `∧`, `∨`, `→`, `∀`, `∃` | The notation would replace the sentence | Grades 7–9 |
| "Valid" as a synonym for "true" | The word would collapse two standards | Grades 7–9 |
| Named fallacies | The name would replace the counterexample | Grades 7–9 |
| Formal probability and base rates | The learner has no population to reason about | Grades 10–12 |
| "Closed-world assumption" as a term | The experience comes before the label | Grade 9 |
| An independent programming environment | The paper base must be secure | Grade 9, supervised |

## The two prohibitions that matter most

**No truth table before the counterexample.** A learner who can fill a truth table and cannot find a case where the premises hold and the conclusion fails has learned a calculation, not a test.

**No independent screen before the paper base.** A learner who can run a program and cannot debug the paper rule base has learned a tool. The tool will not teach her what the paper was for.

# 9. What a school needs to provide

## Time and space

One 45-minute lesson per week, 36 weeks a year. A wall for the rule base that stays up between lessons. A floor space for the search tree, or a large sheet of paper if the floor is not available.

## Materials

- 300 blank cards, A6.
- Masking tape for the search tree.
- Three locks and a set of keys, or a cardboard substitute.
- A family tree large enough to walk.
- One projector and one teacher account for the first screen lesson.

## People

One teacher per class, with one half-day of training on the five-move lesson, the needed/enough distinction, and the screen protocol. A teaching assistant can run the search games. The class teacher must run the rule base and the boundary note, because those require judgement about what the class is ready to name.

## What success looks like

A learner is given a new rule and asks, "What would break it?" A learner is given a search result and asks, "Did we try everything?" A learner is given a headline and asks, "What did they leave out?" Those three questions are the band.

## ^ The standard

- Grice, H. P. "Logic and Conversation." In *Syntax and Semantics 3: Speech Acts*, edited by P. Cole and J. L. Morgan, 41–58. New York: Academic Press, 1975. The maxims as the four questions; the account of *some* and *or* as implicature.
- "Implicature." *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, revised 2024. https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/implicature/. Scalar implicature and the calculability of what is meant but not said.

## ^ Logic and teaching

- Bruner, J. *Toward a Theory of Instruction.* Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1966. The spiral from enactive to iconic to symbolic.
- Van Cleave, M. J. *Introduction to Logic and Critical Thinking,* version 2.0. Chapters 1 and 4, for the teacher's background on conditionals and counterexamples.
- Logical English, LE2. https://le2.logicalcontracts.com. The natural-language rule system used in the first screen lesson.
- SWI-Prolog. https://www.swi-prolog.org. Not used by learners in this band; the teacher may use it to check a rule base.
