# ^ Preface

This is the adult volume. It is for people who have finished formal education and now have to decide things: which supplier, which treatment, which candidate, which policy, which claim to repeat.

It assumes no logic, no statistics and no programming. It assumes something harder to supply: that the learner has sat in a meeting where a confident person was wrong, and has done it herself.

The course has ten sessions of ninety minutes. Each session has a thirty-minute core for lunch-and-learn slots. The aim is not to turn anyone into a logician. It is to install two habits:

> **One decision changed. One question installed.**

The decision is the learner's own, taken during the course. The question is the one she will ask after the course, when no one is reminding her.

The document is standalone. It contains the ten session plans, the thirty-minute versions, the facilitation guide, the decision memo, the Gricean communication session and the references.

# ^ How to use this document

## ^ Who it is for

Anyone who makes or receives claims at work: managers, clinicians, teachers, engineers, civil servants, salespeople, journalists, committee members, parents in a school association.

The group can be mixed. The method depends on mixed experience, not on shared technical background.

## ^ The shape of a session

Every session has the same five parts.

1. **A case.** A real message, decision or number. No definition first.
2. **A question.** One question the case makes urgent.
3. **A try.** Pairs or small groups produce an answer.
4. **A compare.** Two answers are put side by side.
5. **A move.** One sentence or one template the learner can use tomorrow.

The move is the point. A session that ends with a good discussion and no move has failed.

## ^ The thirty-minute version

Each session has a core version for a lunch-and-learn. It keeps the case, the question, the try and the move. It drops the second case and the extended discussion.

## ^ Ground rules

- **Confidentiality.** Cases from the room stay in the room.
- **No cold calling.** Volunteers and pairs, not performances.
- **Evidence over victory.** The aim is a better decision, not a won argument.
- **One move per session.** Do not leave with five.

# ^ The ten sessions at a glance

| # | Session | The question | The move |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The claim and the chain | What is the claim, and what supports it? | Write the claim in one sentence, then the reasons under it |
| 2 | Sources and testimony | Who says so, and how would they know? | Three-source check: who, how, what is left out |
| 3 | Hidden premises | What must be true for this to work? | Write the sentence the speaker did not say |
| 4 | Grice at work | What did they mean, and what did they leave out? | Say what you mean, say enough, say it clearly, ask what was meant |
| 5 | Needed and enough | Is this condition needed, or enough? | Ask: what else would have to be true? |
| 6 | Base rates and risk | How common is it, and what does the test change? | Count people, not percentages |
| 7 | Cause and correlation | Would changing it change the outcome? | Ask: compared with what, and what else changed? |
| 8 | Manipulation and refusal | What is the frame doing? | Name the frame, the omission and the interest |
| 9 | AI at work | What is the chain, and who is accountable? | Use, check, or refuse — and name the check |
| 10 | The decision memo | What will I decide, and what would change my mind? | One page, one owner, one review date |

## ^ What the course does not do

It does not teach formal logic. It does not teach statistics. It does not teach programming. It uses the results of those subjects in the form a decision needs.

It does not promise that a good process produces a good outcome. It promises that the process is defensible when the outcome is bad.

# ^ Session 1 — The claim and the chain

**Purpose.** Separate the claim from the reasons, and notice when there are no reasons.

## ^ The 90-minute plan

| Time | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 | A real email or memo with a recommendation | Start with a case, not a definition |
| 10–25 | Pairs: underline the claim; number the reasons | The claim is often not the first sentence |
| 25–40 | Compare two readings | The same text can carry two claims |
| 40–55 | Input: claim, reason, hidden premise | Three words, one page |
| 55–75 | Reconstruct a second case in standard form | The skill is the reconstruction |
| 75–85 | The move: one sentence, reasons underneath | A template the learner can use |
| 85–90 | Take-home | One real message, reconstructed |

## ^ Core activity

Give each pair a one-page recommendation: a supplier choice, a hiring decision, a policy change. Ask three questions.

1. What is the writer asking me to do?
2. What reasons does she give?
3. Which reason is doing the most work?

The third question is the one that exposes the argument. A recommendation with five reasons often has one load-bearing reason and four decorations.

## ^ The move

> **The claim:** We should ___.
>
> **Because:** 1. ___ 2. ___ 3. ___
>
> **The load-bearing reason:** ___.

## ^ The 30-minute version

Ten minutes on the case, ten on the reconstruction, ten on the move. Skip the second case.

## ^ Take-home

Take one real message from your inbox. Write its claim in one sentence and its reasons underneath. Notice whether the claim was stated or implied.

## ^ Facilitator note

The commonest error is to treat the topic as the claim. *The topic is the new system* is not a claim. *We should buy the new system* is. Ask: what is the writer asking me to do?

# ^ Session 2 — Sources and testimony

**Purpose.** Rank a source by how it knows, not by how confident it sounds.

## ^ The 90-minute plan

| Time | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 | Three reports of one event | The same facts, three frames |
| 10–25 | Source grid: who, how they know, what they leave out | The grid does the work |
| 25–40 | The expert case: right in one domain, out of it in another | Expertise is domain-specific |
| 40–55 | Input: primary, report, repetition | Repetition is not corroboration |
| 55–75 | Re-rank the three reports; write what would change the ranking | The ranking must be revisable |
| 75–85 | The move: three-source check | A portable habit |
| 85–90 | Take-home | One claim, three sources |

## ^ Core activity

The source grid has four columns.

| Source | Who says it | How they know | What they leave out |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | | | |
| B | | | |
| C | | | |

The fourth column is the one learners skip. Ask: what would this source not want you to ask?

## ^ The move

> **Who says it?**
>
> **How do they know?**
>
> **What do they leave out?**
>
> **What would change my mind?**

## ^ The 30-minute version

One claim, two sources, the grid, the move. The expert case becomes a two-minute example.

## ^ Take-home

Take a claim you repeated this week. Find the original source, not the report of it. Write one sentence on what the report left out.

## ^ Facilitator note

Do not let the group rank by trust in the person. Rank by access: who was there, who has the document, who has the measurement. A trusted person with no access is still a weak source.

# ^ Session 3 — Hidden premises

**Purpose.** Find the sentence the argument needs and does not say.

## ^ The 90-minute plan

| Time | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 | A short argument with an obvious gap | The gap is easy to feel, harder to state |
| 10–25 | Pairs write the missing sentence | The sentence must be specific |
| 25–40 | Compare: which missing sentence is load-bearing? | Not every gap matters equally |
| 40–55 | Input: the ghost rule | Write what they did not say, then judge it |
| 55–75 | A policy or business case: three ghosts, rank them | The skill is ranking |
| 75–85 | The move: the ghost sentence | One sentence, one test |
| 85–90 | Take-home | One argument, three ghosts |

## ^ Core activity

The argument:

> We should promote Marta. She has been here the longest.

The hidden premise: *Length of service is the right basis for promotion.* The class writes three more: *She wants the job. She can do the job. No one better is available.* Then rank by load-bearing: which one, if false, changes the decision?

## ^ The move

> **They said:** ___.
>
> **They did not say:** ___.
>
> **If that is false:** ___.

## ^ The 30-minute version

One argument, three ghosts, one ranking. The input is five minutes.

## ^ Take-home

Listen for one recommendation this week. Write the sentence the speaker did not say. Ask, privately, whether it is true.

## ^ Facilitator note

The weak answer is *they assumed it would work*. That is a category, not a sentence. Push for the specific: *the current team can absorb the change without a drop in service*.

# ^ Session 4 — Grice at work

**Purpose.** Say what you mean, say enough, and hear what is meant but not said.

This is the communication session. It is the practical form of Grice's Cooperative Principle, which says that conversation works because the parties assume a shared purpose and contribute what it requires.

## ^ The 90-minute plan

| Time | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 | Three emails: *I'll try*, *some concerns*, *as per my last email* | The implicature is familiar before the theory |
| 10–25 | What is said, what is meant, how do you know? | The inference is calculable |
| 25–40 | The four maxims as four questions | A compact audit |
| 40–55 | Rewrite one email for clarity and one for warmth | Clarity is not coldness |
| 55–70 | The negotiation case: when the other side is not cooperating | The presumption can be exploited |
| 70–85 | The move: four questions before you send | A send-time checklist |
| 85–90 | Take-home | Rewrite one real email |

## ^ The four questions

| Maxim | The question | The failure at work |
|---|---|---|
| Quality | Is it true, and do I have the evidence? | Asserting beyond what you know |
| Quantity | Is it enough, and not too much? | The buried ask; the ten-page update |
| Relation | Does it answer the question? | The meeting that drifts; the reply to a different question |
| Manner | Is it clear, and in the right order? | Acronyms, ambiguity, three asks in one paragraph |

## ^ What is said, what is meant

Three workplace examples.

**"I'll try."** Said about a deadline. It usually means *I do not expect to succeed, and I do not want to refuse.* The words do not say it. The inference comes from the assumption that a cooperative colleague would give a straight answer if she had one.

**"I have some concerns."** Said about a proposal. It usually means *I have more than some, and I am choosing a softer word.* The word *some* implicates *not all*, and in this context *not all* is doing a lot of work.

**"As per my last email."** A factual reference. It usually means *I am frustrated that you did not read it.* The phrase is a flout of Manner: it is unnecessarily formal, and the formality carries the irritation.

## ^ The clarity rewrite

Take a real email and rewrite it with four lines.

> **The ask:** ___
>
> **The reason:** ___
>
> **The deadline:** ___
>
> **What I need from you:** ___

The rewrite is not about being blunt. It is about making the cooperative work possible. A clear email can be warm. An unclear email is not kind; it transfers the work of interpretation to the reader.

## ^ When the other side is not cooperating

Negotiation, sales and internal politics often run on a mixed motive. The speaker wants a deal, not mutual understanding. The cooperative presumption is still triggered, and that is the risk.

The audit asks three questions.

1. What is the ask?
2. What is the interest behind the ask?
3. What would they lose if I understood it fully?

The questions do not make the other side an enemy. They make the cooperative presumption a hypothesis rather than a guarantee.

## ^ The move

Before you send, ask:

> Is it true? Is it enough? Does it answer the question? Is it clear?

Before you believe, ask:

> What did they say? What did they mean? How do I know?

## ^ The 30-minute version

Three emails, the four questions, one rewrite. Skip the negotiation case.

## ^ Take-home

Rewrite one email you have been putting off. Send it. Notice whether the reply is faster.

## ^ Facilitator note

Some groups hear *be clear* as *be cold*. Correct it. Clarity is a service to the reader. The warmth can stay; the ambiguity goes.

# ^ Session 5 — Needed and enough

**Purpose.** Distinguish a condition that is required from a condition that settles the matter.

## ^ The 90-minute plan

| Time | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 | A policy that is necessary but not sufficient | The gap is practical |
| 10–25 | Sort conditions: needed, enough, both, neither | The four boxes |
| 25–40 | Find the case where the needed condition holds and the outcome fails | The counterexample |
| 40–55 | Input: necessary and sufficient, with the key and door | The vocabulary arrives after the sort |
| 55–75 | Apply to a real policy: safety, hiring, procurement | The transfer |
| 75–85 | The move: what else would have to be true? | One question |
| 85–90 | Take-home | One policy, four boxes |

## ^ Core activity

The policy: *All visitors must sign in.* Signing in is needed for security. It is not enough: the door can be propped open, the badge can be borrowed, the list can be unread.

The group sorts the conditions and finds the case where the rule is followed and the outcome still fails. That case is the lesson.

## ^ The move

> **Needed:** ___
>
> **Enough:** ___
>
> **What else would have to be true?** ___

## ^ The 30-minute version

One policy, four boxes, one counterexample, the move.

## ^ Take-home

Take one compliance rule you own. Ask: what is this rule needed for, and what else would have to be true for it to be enough?

## ^ Facilitator note

The commonest error is to treat *necessary* as *sufficient* because the rule is official. The official rule is a condition, not a guarantee. Say it plainly: a key is needed; it is not enough if the door is bolted.

# ^ Session 6 — Base rates and risk

**Purpose.** Count people, not percentages.

## ^ The 90-minute plan

| Time | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 | A test result that most people misread | The intuition fails before the method |
| 10–30 | The 10,000-person table | Counting makes the base rate visible |
| 30–45 | Absolute and relative risk | The same effect, two stories |
| 45–60 | Number needed to treat | The decision number |
| 60–75 | A real screening or fraud case | Transfer |
| 75–85 | The move: three numbers | A portable format |
| 85–90 | Take-home | One claim, three numbers |

## ^ The core case

A disease affects 1 in 100 people. A test is 99% sensitive and 99% specific. A person tests positive. What is the chance she has the disease?

Most people say 99%. The table:

- 10,000 people.
- 100 have the disease. 99 test positive.
- 9,900 do not. 99 test positive falsely.
- 198 positives, 99 with the disease. The chance is 50%.

The lesson is not the formula. It is the denominator. A positive result on a rare condition is often a false alarm, because there are many more healthy people than sick ones.

## ^ The move

> **Out of how many?**
>
> **How many would be wrong?**
>
> **What is the absolute change?**

## ^ The 30-minute version

The 10,000-person table and the three numbers. Skip the fraud case.

## ^ Take-home

Find one percentage in a report you have read. Convert it to people out of 1,000. Write the absolute change.

## ^ Facilitator note

Do not teach Bayes' theorem as a formula. The table is the theorem at this resolution. If someone asks for the formula, give it after the table, not before.

# ^ Session 7 — Cause and correlation

**Purpose.** Ask whether changing the thing would change the outcome.

## ^ The 90-minute plan

| Time | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 | A correlation that invites a causal story | The story is automatic |
| 10–25 | List three alternative explanations | Confounder, reverse cause, coincidence |
| 25–40 | Draw the diagram with one confounder | The picture organises the alternatives |
| 40–55 | Input: intervention, trial, natural experiment | The test is a change |
| 55–75 | A workplace case: training and performance | Transfer |
| 75–85 | The move: compared with what, and what else changed? | Two questions |
| 85–90 | Take-home | One causal claim, three alternatives |

## ^ Core activity

The claim: *Teams that use the new tool perform better.* Three alternatives: better teams choose the tool; the tool is used by teams with more time; the performance measure changed. Then the intervention question: if we assign the tool at random, what would we expect?

## ^ The move

> **Compared with what?**
>
> **What else changed?**
>
> **Would changing it change the outcome?**

## ^ The 30-minute version

One correlation, three alternatives, the move.

## ^ Take-home

Take one causal claim from your organisation. Write the comparison group. If there is none, say so.

## ^ Facilitator note

The commonest error is to accept a mechanism as evidence. A plausible story is not a test. Ask: what would we see if the story were false?

# ^ Session 8 — Manipulation and refusal

**Purpose.** Name the frame, the omission and the interest, and decide what to do.

## ^ The 90-minute plan

| Time | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 | Four cases: debate, advert, threat, lie | The distinctions are practical |
| 10–25 | Sort and define | Manipulation is not the same as lying |
| 25–40 | The hard case: true words, misleading frame | The interesting case |
| 40–55 | Input: the four-way distinction | A shared vocabulary |
| 55–75 | Rewrite a manipulative message honestly | The test of understanding |
| 75–85 | The move: frame, omission, interest | Three questions |
| 85–90 | Take-home | One message, three questions |

## ^ The four cases

- A fair debate: reasons offered, evidence checkable, opponent's view stated fairly.
- An advertisement: a true claim, a selective comparison, an emotional frame.
- A threat: a cost imposed for non-compliance.
- A lie: a statement the speaker believes false, intended to deceive.

The hard case: a true statement, selectively framed, intended to mislead. It is manipulation, not lying. The definition must cover it without covering the fair debate.

## ^ The move

> **What is the frame?**
>
> **What is omitted?**
>
> **Who benefits if I believe it?**

## ^ The 30-minute version

The four cases, the hard case, the move.

## ^ Take-home

Take one message that annoyed you. Name the frame and the omission. Decide whether the annoyance was the manipulation or the message.

## ^ Facilitator note

Do not let the session become a complaint about advertising. The skill is discrimination: not every persuasive message is manipulative, and not every manipulative message is false.

# ^ Session 9 — AI at work

**Purpose.** Use the machine as a draft, a prompt or a search aid, and supply the chain yourself.

## ^ The 90-minute plan

| Time | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 | A fluent machine answer with a factual error | Fluency is not accuracy |
| 10–25 | The five-row audit | The chain, the confidence, the owner |
| 25–40 | Compare two answers to the same question | The model's variability |
| 40–55 | Input: what a language model is and is not | No architecture, just the consequence |
| 55–75 | A workplace task: draft, check, decide | The workflow |
| 75–85 | The move: use, check, refuse | A decision rule |
| 85–90 | Take-home | One task, one audit |

## ^ The five-row audit

| Row | The question |
|---|---|
| Claim | What is the answer asserting? |
| Chain | What would have to be true for it to be right? |
| Missing premise | What is not stated? |
| Confidence | How would the system know? |
| Owner | Who is accountable if it is wrong? |

## ^ The decision rule

- **Use** when the cost of error is low and a check is cheap.
- **Use with a named check** when the cost is material. The check must be executable by a person, with a record.
- **Do not use** when the cost is high and no check is possible.

The rule is not about the technology. It is about the decision.

## ^ The move

> **What is the chain?**
>
> **What is the check?**
>
> **Who owns the answer?**

## ^ The 30-minute version

One answer, the five rows, the decision rule.

## ^ Take-home

Take one task where you used a machine this week. Write the check you did not do. Do it.

## ^ Facilitator note

Do not let the group divide into enthusiasts and refusers. Both are avoiding the audit. The question is not *do you trust AI?* It is *what is this answer worth, and who checks it?*

# ^ Session 10 — The decision memo

**Purpose.** Bring the nine moves together in one page that another person can act on.

## ^ The 90-minute plan

| Time | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 | A one-page memo that changed a decision | The form is the lesson |
| 10–25 | The template: decision, options, evidence, base rate, change, owner, review | Seven boxes |
| 25–40 | Each learner chooses a live decision | The work must be real |
| 40–60 | Draft the memo | Writing is thinking |
| 60–75 | Peer review against the template | The gaps become visible |
| 75–85 | One-minute defence: what would change your mind? | The test of a decision |
| 85–90 | Close: one decision, one question | The course promise |

## ^ The template

> **Decision.** ___
>
> **Options considered.** ___
>
> **Evidence.** ___
>
> **Base rate.** ___
>
> **What would change my mind.** ___
>
> **Owner.** ___
>
> **Review date.** ___

The memo is one page. If it needs two, the decision is not yet clear.

## ^ The defence

Each learner answers one question in one minute:

> What would change your mind?

A decision that cannot be changed by any evidence is not a decision. It is a commitment. The answer must name a specific observation, not a mood.

## ^ The close

Each learner writes two sentences.

> **One decision I will change:** ___
>
> **One question I will keep asking:** ___

The facilitator collects the sentences and, if the group agrees, sends them back in six weeks. The follow-up is the course.

## ^ The 30-minute version

The template, one live decision, the one-minute defence.

## ^ Take-home

Send the memo to one person who will be affected by the decision. Ask for one objection.

## ^ Facilitator note

The commonest failure is a memo that lists evidence without a decision. The template forces a choice. If the learner cannot choose, the decision is not ready; the memo should say what would make it ready.

# ^ Facilitation guide

## ^ Who can facilitate

Someone who can keep time, ask a question and stop a speech. A background in logic helps but is not required. The facilitator's job is not to be the expert. It is to make the group's reasoning visible.

## ^ The room

Tables for pairs and fours. A board. A timer. No podium. The session is a workshop, not a lecture.

## ^ The mixed group

Mix roles and seniority in each pair. The most useful pairs are people who usually do not talk to each other. Do not let the senior person dominate the case; give the pair a task that requires both.

## ^ The difficult moments

**Someone wants a debate.** Offer to test both arguments against the same four questions. The aim is not to settle the politics. It is to make the reasoning visible.

**Someone says "it's all common sense".** Agree, then ask why common sense gets it wrong so often. The course is not a replacement for common sense. It is a set of checks on it.

**Someone is grieving a bad decision.** Do not use their case as an example without permission. The course is about decisions, not about blame.

**Someone wants the formula.** Give it after the table. The formula is a compression of the table, not a substitute for it.

## ^ Assessment

There is no examination. The course is assessed by two things:

- the decision memo, reviewed by a peer against the template;
- the six-week follow-up: did the decision change, and did the question survive?

A certificate that depends on attendance is worth what it costs. A certificate that depends on a memo and a follow-up is worth something.

## ^ What to leave out

Do not teach the history of logic. Do not teach the names of the fallacies unless the group asks. Do not teach probability formulas before the table. Do not teach the architecture of AI. The course is ten moves, not a syllabus.
