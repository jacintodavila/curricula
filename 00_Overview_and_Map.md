# ^ Preface

This is the entry point to the curriculum. It is short on purpose.

The curriculum used to be one document of seventy-two pages. Teachers told us it was too long, too dense, and unclear about which part applied to them. They were right. A document that answers every reader's question at once answers none of them well.

The fix is not to simplify the ideas. It is to give each reader a document that answers the question they actually have. This overview answers one question: **which document do I open, and why?** The four band documents answer the teaching question. The university volumes answer the professional question. The adult volume answers the question of what to do on Monday.

The ideas are unchanged. The resolution is not.

# ^ How to use this document

Read the short version, then the map. Stop when you have found your document.

| If you are... | Read | Then open |
|---|---|---|
| A teacher in grades 1–3 | Sections 2–5, 8–9 | Foundations (G1–3) |
| A teacher in grades 4–6 | Sections 2–5, 8–9 | Rules and Search (G4–6) |
| A teacher in grades 7–9 | Sections 2–5, 8–9 | Critique (G7–9) |
| A teacher in grades 10–12 | Sections 2–5, 8–10 | Judgement (G10–12) |
| A lecturer or department head | Sections 2–7, 9 | T4-CS or T4-GEN |
| A trainer, manager or community facilitator | Sections 2–3, 7–9 | Judgement at Work |
| A principal, inspector or curriculum committee | All of it | The band document for your school |

The filenames are listed in section 6 and in the appendix. The short names above are enough to find the volume.

Three conventions run through the set.

**The band document is standalone.** A teacher should not need this overview, or any other file, to teach from it. Cross-references name the file, but they are optional reading.

**The unit card has a fixed shape.** Question, concepts, what learners do, evidence, language, out of bounds. The shape lets a teacher compare units and lets a committee see where a concept enters and where it is secured.

**The gate decides, not the birthday.** The grade bands are the delivery surface. The gates are the readiness test. A twelve-year-old may be working at band 4–6 resolution on one concept and band 7–9 resolution on another.

# ^ The short version

> **The concepts do not change as students get older. Their resolution changes.**

Implication at eight is a permission rule: *if you ride the bike, you must wear a helmet.* At twelve it is a chain with a necessary and a sufficient condition. At sixteen it is a validity test. At twenty it is a resolution step. At forty it is a clause in a contract nobody read.

The curriculum has four grade bands, each with one organising question and one production task.

| Band | Grades | Ages | Question | The learner makes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Foundations** | 1–3 | 6–9 | Is that the thing, or a name for it? | A question book and a fact wall |
| **Rules and search** | 4–6 | 9–12 | Does it follow? Have we tried everything? | A class rule base and a debug log |
| **Critique** | 7–9 | 12–15 | What did they not say? | A bad-argument rule base and a first program |
| **Judgement** | 10–12 | 15–18 | Where does the model end? | A limit-of-the-model defence and a machine audit |

University and adult life continue the same ladder at professional resolution. They are not a fifth and sixth band of school. They are the same four questions asked with adult consequences.

Grice's theory of implicature is both the editorial standard for these documents and a taught strand. We use it to write clearly. We also teach it, in age-tuned form, because the skill it names — saying enough, saying it clearly, and hearing what is meant but not said — is the skill the whole curriculum exists to build.

# 1. The complaint, and the fix

## What we heard

Four complaints, in the order they were made.

1. **Too long.** A teacher with a free period cannot find the lesson.
2. **Too dense.** Tables of stages, gates, rungs and tracks arrive before the reader knows what any of them is for.
3. **Too many voices.** The document is at once a concept sequence, a curriculum, a handbook and a funding case. Each voice interrupts the others.
4. **Unclear relevance.** A grade 2 teacher reads three chapters before learning that the first useful page is at chapter 13.

The complaints are about quantity and manner, not about truth. The ideas were not wrong. The delivery was.

## The Gricean diagnosis

Grice's Cooperative Principle says: make your contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose of the exchange. A curriculum document has an accepted purpose. It is not to display the design. It is to let a named reader teach a named learner next week.

By that standard, the old document failed three tests.

**Quantity.** It gave more than the reader required. A grade 2 teacher did not need the university capstone, the seven rungs, or the pilot budget. The extra material was not neutral: it hid the useful page.

**Manner.** It was not perspicuous. It used four parallel taxonomies — stages, gates, chapters, tracks — and asked the reader to hold all four at once. Obscurity of expression is a manner failure even when every sentence is clear.

**Relation.** Whole sections were relevant to the project and irrelevant to the reader. Relevance is relative to the accepted purpose. The purpose of a teacher's reading is a lesson, not an approval.

**Quality** was not the problem. The claims were sourced, the distinctions were real, and the warnings were honest. The fix must preserve that.

## The fix

One document per band. Each band document answers four questions and stops:

- What is this band for?
- What does the learner do, year by year?
- How does a teacher teach one unit?
- What evidence shows that it worked?

This overview carries the whole. It is the only document that refers to every other. It is allowed to be short because the detail lives where it is used.

The central seventy-two-page plan is retained as the **design archive**. It explains why the ladder has seventeen stages and why the gates fall where they do. It is not required for teaching. It is the place to go when a committee asks why, not when a teacher asks what.

The concepts that make the curriculum distinctive are unchanged: the closed-world boundary, necessary versus sufficient, the ghost rule, the production task, and logic programming as a mirror rather than a subject. We changed the resolution and the route. The destination is the same.

# 2. The Gricean standard

## The principle

Grice's Cooperative Principle is usually quoted in one sentence:

> Make your contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange in which you are engaged.

A curriculum is a talk exchange with a reader who is not present. The writer must nevertheless imagine the reader's purpose and answer it. The four maxims are the tests.

## The four maxims, as editorial rules

**Quality — make your contribution one that is true.** Do not assert what you believe false. Do not assert what you lack adequate evidence for.

In these documents: every empirical claim is sourced or marked as judgement. Every recommendation is separated from every description. Where the evidence is thin, the document says so. Where a claim is a design choice, it is labelled a design choice.

**Quantity — be as informative as required, and not more.**

In these documents: one idea per paragraph. One job per section. If a paragraph can be cut without changing a decision the reader will make, it is cut. The test is not "is this interesting?" but "does this change what the reader does?"

**Relation — be relevant.**

In these documents: every section states, in its first sentence, the question it answers. Every chapter earns its place by answering a question the reader brought. Material that belongs to another reader lives in another document.

**Manner — be perspicuous. Avoid obscurity and ambiguity. Be brief and orderly.**

In these documents: define a term at first use. Use one name for one thing. Prefer the short sentence. Put the reason next to the claim. Use a table only when the reader needs to compare rows; otherwise use prose.

## The compact test

| Maxim | The writer's test | The reader's symptom when it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Quality | Is this sourced, or labelled as judgement? | "How do you know?" |
| Quantity | Does this change a decision? | "Where is the lesson?" |
| Relation | Does this answer my question? | "Why am I reading this?" |
| Manner | Is it plain, ordered and unambiguous? | "What does this word mean here?" |

## Implicature, and why it matters to a writer

An implicature is what a speaker means without saying. Grice's example: a professor writing a reference says only that a candidate's handwriting is excellent. The reader infers that the candidate is poor at philosophy. Nothing was said. Everything was meant.

The inference is possible because the reader assumes cooperation. If the writer had anything better to report, she would have reported it. The omission carries the message.

A curriculum document generates implicatures too. The strongest is: **this section is what you should teach next.** If the order is unclear, the reader supplies an order of her own, and it is usually wrong. The writer must make the intended inference calculable — that is, derivable from what is on the page, not from what is in the writer's head.

Three rules follow.

1. **Do not rely on omission to carry a message.** If a unit is optional, say so. If a topic is out of bounds, say so.
2. **Put the reason next to the claim.** The reader should not have to reconstruct the warrant from a table three chapters away.
3. **Do not flout a maxim in a document.** Flouting — saying something obviously false or obviously irrelevant in order to imply — is a legitimate move in conversation and a disastrous one in a handbook. The reader cannot see your face. She will take the words literally, or she will give up.

Flouting belongs in the lesson, where it is the object of study. It does not belong in the instructions.

## What the standard does not require

It does not require short documents at any cost, neutral prose, or admiration for the theory. A twenty-page band document that answers every teaching question is better than a five-page one that sends the teacher elsewhere. A curriculum has a point of view; the standard requires that it be visible and argued, not hidden. Grice is used here because he gives the clearest test of whether a document is doing its job. The test would work if his name never appeared.

# 3. The four bands

## The bands

| Band | Grades | Ages | Years | One-sentence promise |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1–3 Foundations** | 1–3 | 6–9 | 3 | The learner can tell a thing from its name, a fact from a guess, and a question from an answer. |
| **4–6 Rules and search** | 4–6 | 9–12 | 3 | The learner can write a rule, test it, search a space, and say what has not been tried. |
| **7–9 Critique** | 7–9 | 12–15 | 3 | The learner can reconstruct an argument, find its hidden premise, and test it against a counterexample. |
| **10–12 Judgement** | 10–12 | 15–18 | 3 | The learner can judge validity, weigh uncertainty, audit a machine answer, and defend a boundary. |

## Why these cut points

The bands follow the four chapters of the curriculum, not the school system: *name it, use it, doubt it, judge it.* The gates fall inside the bands, not at their edges. A learner enters band 4–6 when she can ask a question with a hole in it, which may happen at nine or at eleven. The band gives the teacher a year's worth of material at the right resolution; the gate tells her where to start.

## How the bands map onto school systems

The labels differ; the sequence does not.

| Band | United States | England | International (IB) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | Grades 1–3, elementary | Key Stage 1–2, years 2–4 | PYP, ages 6–9 |
| 4–6 | Grades 4–6, elementary | Key Stage 2, years 5–7 | PYP–MYP, ages 9–12 |
| 7–9 | Grades 7–9, middle school | Key Stage 3, years 8–10 | MYP, ages 12–15 |
| 10–12 | Grades 10–12, high school | Key Stage 4–5, years 11–13 | DP, ages 15–18 |

In systems where elementary school runs to grade 6 and middle school to grade 9, the four bands still apply: 1–3, 4–6, 7–9, 10–12. In systems with a three-year upper secondary, band 10–12 is the whole of it. In systems with a two-year sixth form, band 10–12 includes one year of preparation.

The bands are not a claim about child development. They are a claim about the resolution at which the concepts can be used. A school may run a band across two grades, or split a band across three. The unit cards are the unit of adoption.

# 4. The constant

## Five constants

Across every band, university and adult life, five things do not change.

**The four questions.** What is on the wall? What follows? What do we ask? What is not on the wall? These are the parts of any reasoning artefact: facts, rules, query, boundary. The first three are the program. The fourth is the lesson.

**The four chapters.** Foundations, rules and search, critique, judgement. The names of the bands are the names of the chapters. A teacher in band 1–3 is teaching the first chapter at its lowest resolution. A lecturer is teaching the fourth at its highest.

**The nine gates.** The gates are the readiness tests. They are listed in the design archive and repeated in each band document at the point where they are used. A gate is a question the learner can answer, not a topic she has covered.

**The production task.** Every unit ends in something the learner makes: a card, a rule base, a counterexample, a defence. Recognition is not the skill. A learner who can nod at a validity proof and cannot write a paragraph that survives one has not learned the unit.

**The mirror.** Logic programming — facts, rules, query, edge — is the clearest available way to make reasoning visible. It is not the subject. It is the glass box. The learner can see inside, test it, and change it. That is exactly what a confident chatbot denies.

## The ladder in one paragraph

The learner begins with a thing and its name. She learns that a name is not the thing, and that two names can name one thing. She learns that some statements are true, some false, and some not yet decided. She learns that a question is a hole and a variable is a hole. She learns that a rule connects two facts, and that a rule can be tested by trying to break it. She learns to search a space and to say whether she has tried everything. She learns that a conclusion can be valid and still false, because a premise is false. She learns to find the premise nobody stated. She learns that a model has an edge, and that the edge is where judgement begins.

That paragraph is the whole curriculum. Every document in the set is a different resolution of it.

# 5. The dial

## Six dials

The concepts are constant. Six dials change.

| Dial | 6–9 | 9–12 | 12–15 | 15–18 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Vocabulary** | thing, name, true, guess | rule, enough, search, source | premise, warrant, counterexample | validity, model, uncertainty |
| **Notation** | cards and pictures | paper rules, first LE2 | first Prolog | Prolog, tables, probability |
| **Time horizon** | one lesson | one unit | one term | one year and beyond |
| **Abstraction** | the object in hand | the rule behind it | the form behind the rule | the model behind the form |
| **Social load** | my claim | our class | their claim | the public claim |
| **Machine surface** | none | one screen, supervised | one program, debugged | one system, audited |

## The rule for tuning

Change one dial at a time. A unit that moves vocabulary, notation and social load together will fail, because the learner cannot tell which change caused the difficulty.

The commonest tuning error is to raise the notation before the vocabulary. A twelve-year-old who has not learned to say "what would have to be true for this to be false?" will not learn it by writing a truth table. She will learn the table and lose the question.

The second commonest is to raise the social load before the abstraction. A fourteen-year-old asked to critique a politician's argument before she can critique a classmate's has been asked to do two hard things at once.

## What the dial does not change

It does not change the answer to "what is a good reason?" The standards are the same at six and at sixty: relevance, sufficiency, truth, clarity. What changes is the size of the example, the vocabulary in which the standard is stated, and the consequence of getting it wrong.

# 7. The assessment spine

## One artefact per band

The curriculum does not assess recall unit by unit. It assesses one production per band, plus the learner's ability to explain it.

| Band | The artefact | What it proves | Gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | *My Question Book* and a fact wall explained aloud | The learner can ask, sort and justify a fact | 1–3 |
| 4–6 | A class rule base, a debug log and a source audit | The learner can write, test and repair a rule; can say what has not been tried | 4–6 |
| 7–9 | A bad-argument rule base and a first program with a boundary note | The learner can find the hidden premise and the counterexample; can name what the program does not know | 6–8 |
| 10–12 | A *Limit of the Model* defence and a machine-answer audit | The learner can judge validity, weigh uncertainty, and refuse an unsupported claim | 8–9 |
| University | An argument audit and a program defence | The learner can reconstruct, test and bound a professional claim | 9 |
| Adult | One decision changed and one question installed | The learner can use the move under pressure | 6–9 repair |

A gate marked *seed* in a band is introduced and practised, not secured. The next band secures it. This is why the bands overlap at their edges: a learner can be secure at gate 6 and still seeding gate 7.

## The gate passport

The passport is one page per learner. For each gate it records four things:

1. the question the learner can now ask;
2. the artefact that shows she can ask it;
3. the date and the teacher;
4. the next question.

The passport is not a report card. It is a map of the questions a learner owns. A learner who has gate 5 but not gate 4 is visible on the page, and the teacher knows what to revisit.

## Moderation

Two teachers, one artefact, blind. Each writes one sentence: what the learner can do, and what she cannot yet do. If the sentences disagree, the artefact is ambiguous or the gate is. Fix the artefact or the gate, not the learner.

## What we do not assess

We do not assess the recall of fallacy names. A learner who can name twelve fallacies and cannot find a hidden premise has learned a vocabulary, not a skill.

We do not assess speed. The curriculum is not a race, and the gates are not a league table.

We do not assess confidence. A confident wrong answer and a hesitant right answer are both data; only one is evidence of learning.

# 8. Grice in the classroom

## Why teach Grice

The curriculum asks learners to do four things: say what they mean, mean what they say, say enough, and hear what is meant but not said. That is Grice's subject.

Teaching the theory is not an add-on. It gives the learner a name for the thing she is already doing when she says "that's not what I meant" or "you didn't say that". The name makes the move repeatable.

## The ladder

| Band | What the learner meets | The form it takes |
|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | Four classroom rules | Say true things. Say enough. Say what fits. Say it clearly. |
| 4–6 | The same four as questions | Is it true? Is it enough? Does it fit? Is it clear? |
| 7–9 | Saying versus meaning | Implicature, flouting, scalar implicature, the hidden premise |
| 10–12 | Cooperation versus strategy | When an arguer is not trying to be helpful; implicature in public argument; the machine that sounds cooperative |
| University | Grice as an instrument | Reconstructing implicit premises, reading testimony, auditing a machine answer |
| Adult | Grice under pressure | Meetings, email, negotiation, media, AI assistants |

## The order of teaching

Practice first, name second. A six-year-old learns the four rules by using them, not by hearing the word "maxim". A twelve-year-old learns implicature by catching an advertisement, not by defining "particularized conversational implicature". A sixteen-year-old learns the cooperative principle by analysing a negotiation, not by reciting the definition.

The name arrives when the learner has three examples and needs a handle. That is the same rule as the rest of the curriculum: construct before critique, example before label.

## The AI connection

A language model is a cooperative-sounding text generator with no accountability. It produces sentences that obey the maxims at the surface: relevant, informative, clear, confident. The cooperative presumption that makes ordinary conversation possible is exactly what makes the output persuasive.

Grice gives the learner the question that breaks the spell: **what would the speaker have to know, and how would she know it?** The answer, for a machine, is a training distribution. That is not nothing. It is not a chain.

The audit is the same at sixteen and at forty: ask for the chain, ask what would make it false, ask who is accountable if it is.

# 9. Out of bounds

## The principle

"Out of bounds" does not mean "too hard". It means one of three things:

- the concept has no use at this resolution, so it will be learned as a word;
- the concept will produce a wrong habit that must be unlearned later;
- the concept depends on a gate the learner has not passed.

The band documents state the out-of-bounds list for each year. The table below is the summary.

| Band | Out of bounds | Why | Enters |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | Formal notation; named fallacies; any machine | The word would replace the thing | Band 4–6 |
| 4–6 | Truth tables; Prolog before ten; "valid" for "true" | The table would replace the picture; the syntax would replace the rule | Band 7–9 |
| 7–9 | Cut; formal semantics; base-rate formalism | The control would replace the search; the formalism would replace the judgement | Band 10–12 |
| 10–12 | Completeness proofs; graduate model theory | The proof would replace the use; the theory would replace the defence | University |
| University | None by age; some by prerequisite | The prerequisite is the gate, not the birthday | — |
| Adult | None by age; jargon by choice | The learner decides how much theory she needs | — |

## The two prohibitions that matter most

**No fallacy names before the counterexample.** A learner who can name "affirming the consequent" and cannot construct a counterexample has learned a label that will make her more confident and less accurate.

**No machine before the paper version.** A learner who can run a program and cannot debug the paper rule base has learned a tool, not a discipline. The paper version is not a rehearsal. It is the thing the machine is supposed to make visible.

# 10. What a school needs to decide

## Six decisions

1. **Which band.** Start with the band that matches the learners' gate, not their grade. Use the three diagnostic tasks in the band document.
2. **Who owns it.** One named teacher per band, with time to read the whole document and teach one unit before scaling.
3. **Where it sits.** A strand within an existing subject is enough: English, mathematics, science, citizenship, or a weekly thinking lesson. It does not need a new timetable slot to start.
4. **Which artefact.** Adopt the band artefact as the assessment. Do not add a second assessment in the first year.
5. **What training.** Two half-days: one on the unit card and the lesson cycle, one on the out-of-bounds list and the common errors.
6. **Which pilot.** Two classes, one term, one artefact, one delayed post-test. Compare the artefact with the teacher's judgement, not with a standardised score.

## What success looks like in the first term

A teacher can teach a unit from the band document without asking for another file. A learner can explain one artefact to someone outside the class. The head can see, on one page, which gates the cohort owns and which it does not.

That is enough for a first term. The rest is repetition.
