# ^ Preface

This is the curriculum for grades 7–9. It is for young people who can argue, and who have discovered that arguing well is not the same as being right.

The band has one job: **the learner can reconstruct an argument, find its hidden premise, and test it against a counterexample.** The three years build that job from standard form to the first program and the first machine audit.

The document is standalone. It contains the year map, eighteen unit cards, a scripted lesson on implicature, the first-program protocol, the assessment, the common errors and the out-of-bounds list.

Three principles shape the band.

**The counterexample comes before the fallacy name.** A learner who can construct a case where the premises hold and the conclusion fails has a tool. A learner who can name twelve fallacies has a vocabulary.

**Saying and meaning are different, and the difference is teachable.** This is the band where Grice's implicature becomes explicit. The learner already knows how to hear a hint. She learns why the hint works, when it is exploited, and how to refuse it.

**The machine is a witness, not a judge.** The first program shows what follows from the facts entered. It cannot show what is missing. The learner writes the boundary note before she writes the program's defence.

# ^ How to use this document

## ^ The shape of the year

One 50-minute lesson each week, for 36 weeks. Three years, 108 lessons. The lessons sit inside English, civics, science, religious studies, philosophy or a weekly critical-thinking slot.

Each unit is six lessons. The sixth lesson is the audit: the learner presents the artefact and answers three questions — *what does it claim, what does it leave out, and what would change your mind?*

## ^ The shape of a unit card

**Question.** The one question the unit teaches the learner to ask.

**Do.** Three activities that make the question operational.

**Words.** The vocabulary the learner should be able to use by the end.

**Evidence.** What the teacher keeps, and what it shows.

**Out of bounds.** What not to introduce in this unit, even if a learner asks.

## ^ The five-move lesson, at this resolution

The lesson shape is unchanged: a thing in the hand, a question with a gap, a try, a compare, a name. What changes is the thing.

The "thing in the hand" is now a real argument: a headline, an advertisement, a policy, a court judgment, a classmate's speech. The "name" is a distinction the class has already used three times: *said* versus *meant*, *valid* versus *true*, *not provable* versus *false*.

The teacher's discipline is the same: do not name the distinction until the class has three examples and needs the handle.

## ^ What the learner can do at the end

A learner who completes the band can:

- put a real argument into standard form, with premises and conclusion;
- distinguish validity from truth, and say why a valid argument can have a false conclusion;
- construct a counterexample, including a hypothetical one;
- identify a hidden premise and say whether it is plausible;
- explain the difference between what a speaker said and what she meant;
- recognise a scalar implicature and cancel it;
- explain flouting, and why irony and hyperbole are not lies;
- name the four-way distinction between persuasion, manipulation, coercion and lying;
- write a small Prolog program with facts, rules and a query;
- trace a recursive query and explain where it stops;
- explain why the machine's *no* means *not provable*, not *false*;
- audit a fluent machine answer and ask for the chain.

## ^ What the band does not do

It does not teach cut. It does not teach formal semantics. It does not teach base-rate formalism before age fifteen. It does not teach completeness or soundness proofs. It does not name a fallacy without a counterexample in the same lesson.

# ^ The Grice turn

## ^ Why this band

Grades 1–3 taught four rules. Grades 4–6 turned them into four questions and showed that *some* does not mean *all*. This band explains why.

The explanation is Grice's. Conversation is a cooperative activity. Speakers and listeners assume a shared purpose. On that assumption, a listener can infer more than the words say. The inference is not a guess. It is a calculation from three things: the words, the situation, and the assumption that the speaker is trying to be helpful.

Grice called the inferred meaning an **implicature**. The word is ugly. The thing is familiar.

## ^ The Cooperative Principle

> Make your contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange in which you are engaged.

In a classroom: say enough for the listener to understand, at the point where she needs it, for the purpose you are both pursuing.

The four maxims are the parts of the principle.

- **Quality.** Make your contribution true. Do not say what you believe false. Do not say what you lack evidence for.
- **Quantity.** Be as informative as required. Do not say more than required.
- **Relation.** Be relevant.
- **Manner.** Be perspicuous. Avoid obscurity and ambiguity. Be brief and orderly.

## ^ The reference letter

Grice's own example is the cleanest teaching case.

> A student applies for a lectureship in philosophy. His referee writes: *His handwriting is excellent.*

What does the letter mean? It means the candidate is poor at philosophy. Nothing in the sentence says so. The reader infers it because a cooperative referee would mention philosophy if she could. The omission is the message.

This is a **particularized conversational implicature**: it depends on the situation. In a handwriting competition, the same sentence would mean something else.

## ^ Scalar implicature

> Some of the students passed.

The learner hears: not all. The word *some* does not logically exclude *all*. The inference comes from Quantity: if all had passed, a cooperative speaker would have said *all*. The implicature is **generalized** because it arises by default, without a special situation.

It is also **cancellable**:

> Some of the students passed — in fact, all of them did.

The cancellation works because the first sentence never entailed *not all*. If it had, the second sentence would contradict the first.

## ^ Flouting

A speaker **flouts** a maxim when she deliberately and openly breaks it, expecting the listener to infer the point.

- *War is war.* A tautology flouts Quantity. The implicature: war is terrible, and we should not expect it to be otherwise.
- *What a lovely day*, said in a downpour. Flouts Quality. The implicature: the opposite, or resignation.
- *I have nothing to say*, said while obviously furious. Flouts Quantity. The implicature: something is very wrong.

Flouting is not lying. A lie hides the breach. A flout displays it. The listener is invited to see the gap and fill it.

## ^ Why it matters

Three reasons, in order of urgency for a fourteen-year-old.

**Deniability.** A speaker can say something false in implication and true in words. *I never said he was a bad philosopher.* The audit must reconstruct what was meant, not only what was said.

**Manipulation.** An advertisement says *probably the best*. The word *probably* is doing work: it sounds like a guarantee and legally is not one. The implicature is stronger than the claim.

**The machine.** A language model produces fluent, cooperative-sounding text. It obeys the maxims at the surface: relevant, informative, clear, confident. The cooperative presumption that makes ordinary talk work is exactly what makes the output persuasive. The audit asks: **what would the speaker have to know, and how would she know it?**

## ^ What not to do

Do not teach the taxonomy before the examples. Do not ask a fourteen-year-old to define "particularized conversational implicature" before she has caught three advertisements. Do not treat implicature as a form of lying. It is a form of meaning. It can be used honestly or dishonestly, and the difference is the next lesson.

# 1. Year 1 — Argument and form

## ^ The year's question

What is the argument, and what holds it together?

The first year gives the learner the apparatus: claim, grounds, warrant; standard form; validity and counterexample; the difference between true and valid; the hidden premise. It ends with consistency, because a set of claims can fail together even when each claim looks fine.

## What is an argument?

**Question.** What is the difference between an argument and a fight?

**Do.** Two transcripts: a quarrel and a reasoned disagreement. The class marks the claim each person is trying to establish, the reasons offered, and the point where a reason is missing. Then a one-sentence argument from a news report. What is the claim? What is offered in support?

**Words.** claim, reason, conclusion, premise, support, argument, quarrel.

**Evidence.** The learner marks the claim and two reasons in a short transcript.

**Out of bounds.** Do not introduce Toulmin's full schema yet. Claim and reason are enough; the warrant arrives in unit 1.5.

## Standard form

**Question.** How do we put an argument on one page?

**Do.** Rewrite three arguments as numbered premises and a conclusion. Remove the rhetoric. Keep the logical connectives. Then reverse: give a standard form to a partner and ask her to write it as a paragraph. Compare. Did the paragraph add anything?

**Words.** premise, conclusion, standard form, therefore, because, since.

**Evidence.** Three arguments in standard form, with the rhetoric removed.

**Out of bounds.** Do not require a symbolic notation. Numbers and words are enough.

## Valid and invalid

**Question.** What would have to be true for the conclusion to be false while the premises are true?

**Do.** Give a valid argument and an invalid one. Ask the countermodel question for each. Draw the invalid case as a small world: two boxes, three people, a table. The drawing is the counterexample. Then a valid argument: try to draw the counterexample. The failure to draw one is not a proof, but it is the beginning of one.

**Words.** valid, invalid, counterexample, model, premises, conclusion, all, some.

**Evidence.** The learner draws a counterexample to an invalid argument and explains why the valid one has none.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use truth tables. Do not say "valid" means "true".

## True and valid are different

**Question.** Can a valid argument have a false conclusion?

**Do.** The argument: *All teachers are rich. Ms Silva is a teacher. Therefore Ms Silva is rich.* Valid or invalid? Valid. True or false? The conclusion is false because the first premise is false. The form is good; the input is bad. Then the reverse: a true conclusion from an invalid argument.

**Words.** valid, sound, true, false, premise, conclusion, form, content.

**Evidence.** The learner sorts four arguments into *valid and true*, *valid and false*, *invalid and true*, *invalid and false*.

**Out of bounds.** Do not teach the full soundness definition until the class has the four cases. Then name it once.

## The hidden premise

**Question.** What must be true for the argument to work?

**Do.** The argument: *She is a doctor, so she will be able to help with your leg.* The hidden premise: *Doctors can help with legs.* Then a policy argument: *We need the rule because safety.* Hidden premises: *The rule improves safety. The cost is acceptable. There is no better rule.* Rank the hidden premises by plausibility.

**Words.** hidden premise, assumption, warrant, must be true, plausible, support.

**Evidence.** The learner writes two hidden premises for one argument and ranks them.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use "enthymeme". Hidden premise is clearer and does the same work.

## Consistency

**Question.** Can a set of claims be individually plausible and jointly impossible?

**Do.** A set of five policy claims. Find the pair that cannot both be true. Then a set where the contradiction is three steps away: A implies B, B implies not C, and C is asserted. The learner draws the chain. The contradiction is a property of the set, not of any single claim.

**Words.** consistent, inconsistent, contradiction, together, set, imply, cannot both.

**Evidence.** The learner finds a contradiction in a set and shows the chain.

**Out of bounds.** Do not teach paraconsistent logic. The lesson is that a set can fail together.

# 2. Year 2 — Critique and meaning

## ^ The year's question

What is said, what is meant, and who benefits?

The second year is the heart of the band. The learner moves from the form of the argument to its surface: the words that carry more than they say, the patterns that look like reasons and are not, and the interests behind the message.

## Fallacies as questions

**Question.** What question exposes this bad argument?

**Do.** Five bad arguments, no names. For each, the learner writes the question that exposes it: *Compared with what? Who says? What else changed? Does the conclusion follow? What is the hidden premise?* Only then does the teacher give the conventional name, as a label for the question the class already asked.

**Words.** question, pattern, assumption, comparison, source, consequence, name.

**Evidence.** The learner matches five bad arguments to five questions, without using a fallacy name.

**Out of bounds.** Do not begin with a list of names. Do not test the names in isolation.

## The converse and the inverse

**Question.** Does the rule work backwards?

**Do.** The rule: *If it rains, the match is cancelled.* It rains; the match is cancelled. The match is cancelled; did it rain? Not necessarily. The learner draws the case: the match was cancelled for lightning. Then the inverse: no rain; is the match on? Not necessarily. Two errors, one picture each.

**Words.** converse, inverse, backwards, not necessarily, counterexample, rule.

**Evidence.** The learner draws a counterexample to the converse and states what the rule does not say.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use the Latin names until the pictures are secure. Then name them once.

## Saying and meaning

**Question.** What did they mean that they did not say?

**Do.** The reference letter. The tray of cups. *Some*, *or*, *if*. The learner reconstructs the inference: what was said, what was assumed, what was meant. Then she cancels an implicature: *Some passed — in fact, all did.* What happens to the inference?

**Words.** said, meant, implicature, infer, cancel, some, all, or.

**Evidence.** The learner writes a said/meant/cancelled table for three examples.

**Out of bounds.** Do not taxonomise implicature into four types. Particularized, generalized and scalar are enough, and only after the examples.

## Flouting

**Question.** Why say something obviously false or irrelevant?

**Do.** *War is war.* *What a lovely day* in a storm. *I have nothing to say*, said while furious. The learner identifies the maxim that was broken, the gap between words and situation, and the meaning the gap produces. Then an advertisement that flouts: a car shown on an empty road, with the line *For people who take the scenic route.*

**Words.** flout, irony, hyperbole, tautology, gap, meaning, intention.

**Evidence.** The learner explains one flout: which maxim, what gap, what meaning.

**Out of bounds.** Do not teach the full Gricean apparatus of maxim-flouting pairs. One clear example of each is enough.

## Manipulation and its neighbours

**Question.** What is the difference between persuasion, manipulation, coercion and lying?

**Do.** Four cases: a fair debate; an advertisement that hides the cost; a threat; a false statement. Sort them. Then the hard case: a true statement, selectively framed, intended to mislead. Where does it sit? The learner writes a definition of manipulation that covers the hard case without covering the fair debate.

**Words.** persuasion, manipulation, coercion, lying, frame, omit, intend, mislead.

**Evidence.** The learner sorts four cases and defends one borderline judgement.

**Out of bounds.** Do not reduce manipulation to lying. The distinction is the lesson.

## The argument audit

**Question.** Can I take an argument apart and put it back together?

**Do.** The learner chooses a real argument — a column, a speech, a policy, a court judgment. She reconstructs it in standard form, names the hidden premise, offers a counterexample or a counter-consideration, and writes a verdict: *strong*, *weak*, *valid but unsound*, *not an argument*. Then she writes the strongest reply to her own verdict.

**Words.** audit, reconstruction, hidden premise, counterexample, verdict, reply, strong, weak.

**Evidence.** The audit, with a reply to the learner's own verdict. This is the year 2 artefact.

**Out of bounds.** Do not require a grade for the verdict. The quality is in the reconstruction and the reply.

# 3. Year 3 — Machines and the edge

## ^ The year's question

What does the machine know, and what does it not?

The third year introduces logic programming. The purpose is not to produce programmers. It is to make the learner's own reasoning visible in a form she can run, break and bound. The year ends with the AI audit, where the same questions are asked of a system that does not show its rules.

## First Prolog: facts, rules, queries

**Question.** What are the three parts of a program?

**Do.** Read a program before writing one.

```prolog
parent(alice, bob).
parent(bob, carol).

ancestor(X, Y) :- parent(X, Y).
ancestor(X, Y) :- parent(X, Z), ancestor(Z, Y).
```

The class labels the facts, the rules and the query. Then the teacher asks: `?- ancestor(alice, carol).` The class predicts. The teacher runs. The class traces the answer by hand.

**Words.** fact, rule, query, predicate, argument, program, run, true, false.

**Evidence.** The learner labels a program and predicts one query.

**Out of bounds.** Do not teach cut. Do not teach negation yet. Do not let the syntax arrive before the paper rule base.

## Unification: filling the hole

**Question.** How does the machine fill a variable?

**Do.** The query `?- parent(alice, X).` The machine finds `X = bob`. Show the substitution. Then a query with two variables. Then a query that fails. The learner writes the substitution by hand before the machine does it. The variable is the hole from grade 3, now with a name.

**Words.** variable, substitute, unify, match, bind, fail, X.

**Evidence.** The learner writes the substitution for three queries before running them.

**Out of bounds.** Do not teach the occurs check. Do not teach the resolution algorithm formally.

## Recursion and search

**Question.** How does the machine search a recursive rule?

**Do.** Trace `?- ancestor(alice, X).` Draw the tree. Mark the choice points. Find the base case. Then remove the base case and run again: the program loops or fails. The learner sees the floor from grade 5 in a new material.

**Words.** recursion, base case, choice point, trace, tree, loop, stop.

**Evidence.** The learner draws the trace tree and marks the base case.

**Out of bounds.** Do not teach tabling or memoisation. Do not teach SLD resolution as a formal system.

## Negation as failure

**Question.** What does the machine mean when it says no?

**Do.** The program has a parent fact for Alice and Bob, but none for Alice and Carol. The query about Alice and Carol fails. The class asks: is it false that Alice is Carol's parent, or is it just not in the program? Add the fact. Run again. The *no* was not a fact about the world. It was a fact about the program.

**Words.** negation, failure, not provable, not in the program, no, unknown, add.

**Evidence.** The learner explains the difference between *false* and *not provable* for one query.

**Out of bounds.** Do not teach the semantics of negation as failure. Do not teach stable models. The distinction is the lesson.

## The closed world and the boundary note

**Question.** What is the edge of the program?

**Do.** The class writes a boundary note for the ancestor program: what it knows (the parent facts), what it can derive (ancestors), what it cannot know (whether a missing parent is unknown or absent), and what it cannot decide (whether a person is alive). Then the same note for a school-trip rule base. The note is the assessment.

**Words.** closed world, boundary, edge, missing, unknown, absent, cannot decide.

**Evidence.** A boundary note with four boxes: knows, derives, cannot know, cannot decide.

**Out of bounds.** Do not teach the closed-world assumption as a formal semantic definition. The four boxes are enough.

## The AI audit

**Question.** What does a fluent answer leave out?

**Do.** Give the class a machine answer to a real question — a summary, a recommendation, a medical or legal claim. The learner asks: What is the claim? What is the chain? Which premise is missing? What would make it false? Who is accountable? Then she writes a verdict: *use*, *use with a check*, *do not use*.

**Words.** chain, premise, missing, false, accountable, check, verdict, fluent.

**Evidence.** The AI audit, with one named check and one named owner. This is the band artefact.

**Out of bounds.** Do not teach the architecture of a language model. Do not teach training data or transformers. The audit is about the answer, not the engineering.

## The first-program protocol

Use the same six steps every time.

1. **Paper first.** The class writes the facts and rules on cards.
2. **Predict.** Each learner writes the answer and the substitution before anything runs.
3. **Run one query.** The teacher runs it; the class watches.
4. **Trace.** The class draws the tree and marks the base case.
5. **Break it.** Remove one fact or the base case. Predict, run, explain.
6. **Bound it.** Write the four-box boundary note.

The protocol is the assessment. A learner who can predict and then explain a surprise has the concept. A learner who can only read the output has a demonstration.

# 4. A scripted lesson: saying and meaning

**Year 2, units 2.3 and 2.4. Time: 50 minutes. Group: 24 learners.**

**Question.** What did they mean that they did not say?

**Materials.** Three cards: a reference letter, a headline, an advertisement. A board divided into *said*, *assumed*, *meant*.

## ^ Minute 0–8: the letter

The teacher reads the letter aloud.

> To whom it may concern. I have known Tomas for four years. His handwriting is excellent. He is punctual. Yours sincerely, Dr Pereira.

> **Teacher:** Would you hire Tomas to teach philosophy?

The class says no. The teacher asks why. The class says the letter is faint praise. The teacher writes *His handwriting is excellent* under *said*.

> **Teacher:** Where does "he is poor at philosophy" appear in the letter?

Nowhere. The class has inferred it. The teacher writes it under *meant*.

> **Teacher:** What did you assume to get there?

The class says: if he were good, she would have said so. The teacher writes *a good referee mentions the relevant thing* under *assumed*.

> **Teacher:** That assumption is the Cooperative Principle. We assume the writer is trying to be helpful. On that assumption, the omission speaks.

## ^ Minute 8–18: the calculation

The teacher draws the calculation on the board.

1. The writer said only *handwriting* and *punctuality*.
2. A helpful referee would mention teaching ability if she could.
3. She did not.
4. Therefore, she cannot, or she would not.
5. Therefore, the candidate is poor at philosophy.

> **Teacher:** Is this a guess?

No. It is an inference from the words plus the assumption of cooperation. It can be wrong. The writer may have been rushed, or may have been told to write only those facts. That is why the inference is cancellable.

The teacher adds a second paragraph to the letter: *I am not permitted to comment on his philosophy.* The implicature changes. The class sees that the same words can mean different things when the situation changes.

## ^ Minute 18–28: some and all

The teacher writes:

> Some of the students passed.

> **Teacher:** Did all pass?

The class says no. The teacher asks: does the word *some* mean *not all*?

The class is divided. The teacher writes the cancellation:

> Some of the students passed — in fact, all of them did.

> **Teacher:** Is this a contradiction?

No. The first sentence is still true. The second cancels the inference. The word *some* did not entail *not all*. The listener inferred it.

The teacher names the pattern: **scalar implicature**. The scale is *none, some, all*. Saying a weaker word implicates that the stronger word is not true, because a cooperative speaker would have used the stronger one.

## ^ Minute 28–38: the headline

The teacher shows the headline:

> **Crime doubles in city schools.**

> **Teacher:** What is said?

Crime doubled. The teacher writes it.

> **Teacher:** What is meant?

That schools are more dangerous than before. The teacher writes it.

> **Teacher:** What is assumed?

That the comparison is meaningful. The teacher writes it. Then the class asks the questions:

- Doubled from what? Two cases to four is a doubling.
- Which schools? All of them, or one?
- Which crimes? Theft, assault, or a category that changed?
- Over what period? A year, a month, a week?

The teacher reveals the small print: *reported incidents of bicycle theft in three schools, compared with the same month last year.* The headline is true. The implicature is misleading. The class decides which is the bigger problem: the words or the inference.

## ^ Minute 38–46: the advertisement

The teacher shows an advertisement:

> **Probably the best coffee in town.**

> **Teacher:** What does *probably* do?

The class says it makes the claim weaker. The teacher asks: why would an advertiser weaken a claim? The class says: so it cannot be proved false. The word *probably* is a shield. The implicature — *the best* — is stronger than the claim.

The class writes the audit sentence:

> The advertisement says *probably*; it means *best*; it can defend itself by pointing to *probably*.

## ^ Minute 46–50: exit ticket

Each learner writes three lines.

> **Said:** ___
>
> **Meant:** ___
>
> **Clue:** ___

The teacher collects the tickets and sorts them into three piles: *said only*, *meant with a clue*, *confused*. The piles set the next lesson.

## ^ What the teacher watches for

- A learner who says "the letter says he is bad" has collapsed saying into meaning. Ask: point to the words.
- A learner who says "the letter says nothing about philosophy" has the said/meant gap but not the inference. Ask: why does the omission matter?
- A learner who says "it depends on who wrote it" is ready for particularized implicature. Ask: what would change if the writer were his mother?

## ^ What not to do

Do not begin with the definitions. Do not ask the class to memorise *particularized*, *generalized* and *scalar* before they have caught three examples. Do not treat the headline as a lie. It is true. The lesson is that truth is not the only standard.

# 5. Assessment

## The three artefacts

**The standard-form reconstruction.** One real argument, premises and conclusion, with the rhetoric removed. The learner marks one premise as *stated* and one as *hidden*.

**The bad-argument rule base.** Six rules that identify a bad pattern, each with a question and a counterexample. One rule must have an exception, because not every instance of the pattern is bad. The learner explains why the exception is there.

**The first program.** A small Prolog program with at least three facts and one rule. A query, a trace, and a four-box boundary note. The program does not need to be original. The boundary note must be.

**The argument audit.** One real argument, reconstructed, attacked, defended, and judged. The verdict must name what would change the learner's mind.

## The gate descriptors

| Gate | The learner can... | The evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | reconstruct an argument in standard form | premises and conclusion, with one hidden premise marked |
| 6 | construct a counterexample | a drawn or described case where the premises hold and the conclusion fails |
| 7 | distinguish validity from truth | the four-cell sort, with an example in each cell |
| 7 | explain an implicature | said/assumed/meant for one example, with a cancellation |
| 7 | explain a flout | which maxim, what gap, what meaning |
| 8 (seed) | write and trace a small program | facts, rule, query, trace tree, base case |
| 8 (seed) | explain the machine's *no* | *not provable* versus *false*, with one added fact |
| 8 (seed) | audit a machine answer | claim, chain, missing premise, check, owner |

## Moderation

Two teachers, one artefact, blind. Each writes one sentence: what the learner can do, and what she cannot yet do. If the sentences disagree, the task is ambiguous. Fix the task.

## What not to assess

Do not assess the recall of fallacy names. Do not assess the elegance of the Prolog code. Do not assess whether the learner agrees with the teacher's verdict on a real argument. Assess the reconstruction, the counterexample and the boundary note.

# 6. Common errors, and what to do

| What the learner says | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| "It's valid, so it's true." | Validity and truth collapsed | The rich-teacher argument; valid form, false premise |
| "A counterexample has to be real." | The method is mistaken for history | Draw a possible world; validity is about all possibilities |
| "The premise is false, so the argument is invalid." | Bad input confused with bad form | Separate the two verdicts; mark each |
| "An implicature is a lie." | Meaning confused with deception | The reference letter; true words, misleading omission |
| "Some means not all." | The implicature treated as an entailment | Cancel it: *some — in fact, all* |
| "The machine said no, so it's false." | Closed world treated as truth | Add the missing fact; run again |
| "Negation as failure is negation." | The procedure confused with the logic | *Not provable* versus *false*, with the four boxes |
| "I named the fallacy, so I won." | The label replaced the argument | Ask for the counterexample; no name without a case |

## The one error to pre-empt

**Valid means true.** It is the error that makes a clever person trust a bad argument because it is well formed. The correction is a sentence the learner can use for the rest of her life: **the form can be good and the premises false.** Say it once a week until it is boring.

# 7. Out of bounds

| Not this | Why not yet | Where it enters |
|---|---|---|
| Cut | The control would replace the search | Grades 10–12 |
| Formal semantics and model theory | The definition would replace the picture | Grades 10–12, university |
| Base-rate formalism | The learner has no population to reason about | Grades 10–12 |
| Completeness and soundness proofs | The proof would replace the use | University |
| Modal logic | The vocabulary would arrive before the need | University |
| The full taxonomy of implicature | The labels would replace the examples | University |
| An independent AI system without a protocol | The audit must be taught before the tool is used | Grades 10–12 |

## The two prohibitions that matter most

**No fallacy name without a counterexample.** A learner who can name *affirming the consequent* and cannot draw the case has learned a label that will make her more confident and less accurate.

**No machine answer without a boundary note.** A learner who can prompt a system and cannot say what it does not know has learned a trick. The note is the discipline.

# 8. What a school needs to provide

## Time and space

One 50-minute lesson per week, 36 weeks a year. A room with a board and space for pairs. A set of real arguments that the teacher refreshes each term: three headlines, two advertisements, one policy, one speech.

## Materials

- A folder of argument packs, one per unit.
- A set of six maxim cards for the wall.
- One computer and projector for the first program; one device per pair for the AI audit, supervised.
- A class set of the audit template.

## People

One teacher per class, with one day of training on standard form, counterexamples, implicature and the first-program protocol. The teacher does not need to be a programmer. She needs to be able to read a trace and say what the program does not know.

## What success looks like

A learner hears a claim and asks, "What is the hidden premise?" A learner hears a headline and asks, "Compared with what?" A learner runs a program and asks, "What is not in it?" Those three questions are the band.
