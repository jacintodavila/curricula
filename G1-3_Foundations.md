# ^ Preface

This is the curriculum for grades 1–3. It is for children who are learning to read, to take turns, and to tell the difference between what happened and what they wish had happened.

The band has one job: **the learner can tell a thing from its name, a fact from a guess, and a question from an answer.** Everything else in the three years serves that job.

The document is standalone. A teacher can teach from it without opening another file. It contains the year map, eighteen unit cards, a scripted lesson, the assessment, the common errors and the out-of-bounds list.

Three principles shape the band.

**The object comes before the word.** A child meets sameness by sorting buttons, not by defining "identity". She meets a rule by keeping a classroom rule, not by writing a conditional.

**The question comes before the answer.** A child who can ask "how do you know?" has a tool that survives every later subject. A child who can only answer has a performance.

**The machine waits.** There is no computer in this band. The first fact wall is made of cards, and the first rule base is made of paper. The machine enters in grades 4–6, when the paper version is secure.

# ^ How to use this document

## ^ The shape of the year

One 40-minute lesson each week, for 36 weeks. Three years, 108 lessons. The lessons sit inside an existing subject — literacy, numeracy, science, citizenship or a weekly circle — rather than in a new timetable slot.

Each unit is six lessons. The sixth lesson is always the same: the learner explains the artefact to someone outside the class. That explanation is the assessment.

Five minutes of every lesson is talk practice. The four classroom rules are not a poster. They are the routine.

## ^ The shape of a unit card

Every unit card has the same parts.

**Question.** The one question the unit teaches the learner to ask.

**Do.** Three activities that make the question operational. They are not a script; they are the minimum.

**Words.** The vocabulary the learner should be able to use by the end. Not define. Use.

**Evidence.** What the teacher keeps, and what it shows.

**Out of bounds.** What not to introduce in this unit, even if a child asks.

## ^ The shape of a lesson

Every lesson has five moves.

1. **A thing in the hand.** An object, a picture or a story. No definition first.
2. **A question with a gap.** The teacher asks something the class cannot answer by recall.
3. **A try.** Pairs or small groups make an answer with the materials.
4. **A compare.** Two or three answers are put side by side. The class says how they differ.
5. **A name.** The teacher gives the word for what the class just did, and only then.

The five moves are the same at every band. The resolution changes.

## ^ What the learner can do at the end

A learner who completes the band can:

- give two names for one thing and say that both are names;
- sort the same set by two different properties, and say why the sort changed;
- mark a statement true, false or not yet decided, and give a reason;
- say where a fact came from: I saw it, someone told me, or I guessed;
- ask a question with a hole in it — *which one?*, *where?*, *who?*;
- follow a rule with an exception;
- search a small set and say whether she has looked everywhere;
- explain a three-rule fact base to an adult without using the word "logic".

## ^ What the band does not do

It does not teach formal notation. It does not name fallacies. It does not use truth tables. It does not put a child in front of a programming environment. Those belong to later bands, and introducing them here would produce the appearance of learning without the habit.

# ^ The four classroom rules

Grice's Cooperative Principle says that conversation works because speakers and listeners assume a shared purpose and try to contribute what it requires. The full theory belongs to the teacher, not the child. The child meets it as four rules.

1. **Say true things.**
2. **Say enough.**
3. **Say what fits.**
4. **Say it clearly.**

These are the band's version of Quality, Quantity, Relation and Manner. They are taught by use, one at a time, and then together.

## ^ Rule 1 — Say true things

The child learns the difference between:

- **I saw it.** She was there.
- **Someone told me.** She trusts the teller, or does not.
- **I guessed.** She made it up to fill a gap.

A guess is not a lie. A lie is a statement the speaker believes false, made to deceive. A guess is a statement the speaker does not know, offered as a try. The distinction is the beginning of intellectual honesty, and it is teachable at six.

**Practice.** The teacher holds a covered box. Children say what is in it. Each answer is marked with a card: *saw*, *told*, *guessed*. The box is opened. The guesses are not punished. They are labelled.

## ^ Rule 2 — Say enough

The child learns that a listener needs enough to act.

**Too little:** "It's over there."
**Enough:** "It's on the shelf, next to the blue box."
**Too much:** the whole history of the shelf.

The test is practical: can the listener find it? The teacher plays the listener and acts on exactly what was said. If the listener goes to the wrong place, the instruction was too small. If the listener stops listening, it was too large.

## ^ Rule 3 — Say what fits

The child learns that an answer can be true and still not answer the question.

**Question:** "Where is the red pencil?"
**Answer:** "I like red."

The answer is true. It does not fit. The teacher does not say "wrong". She says: "That is true. Does it answer my question?" The phrase *what has that got to do with it?* becomes a classroom routine, asked kindly.

## ^ Rule 4 — Say it clearly

The child learns that order and naming are part of the message.

**Unclear:** "Put it with the other one after the thing."
**Clear:** "Put the small cup on the top shelf, next to the big cup."

The child learns three tools: name the thing, say the order, say one thing at a time. The tools are practised in giving instructions, not in labelling sentences.

## ^ What did they mean?

At this age, the child can meet implicature without the word. An implicature is what a speaker means without saying.

Three classroom examples:

- *The door is open.* Said while the teacher looks at the door. It may mean: close it.
- *Some children have finished.* It usually means: not all have finished.
- *It is cold in here.* It may mean: please close the window.

The teacher asks: **What did I say? What did I mean? How did you know?** The third question is the important one. The child learns that meaning is inferred from the words, the situation and the assumption that the speaker is trying to help.

Do not teach the word "implicature" in this band. Teach the question. The word arrives in grades 7–9, when the learner has a shelf of examples to hang it on.

# 1. Year 1 — Naming and sorting

## ^ The year's question

What is this, and what is it called?

The first year is about the gap between a thing and its name. Children arrive knowing many names. They do not yet know that a name is not the thing, that one thing can have several names, and that two people can use different names for the same thing without disagreeing about the world.

The year also introduces the three-way sort: true, false, not sure. This is the child's first logical instrument, and it is more important than any definition.

## Same and different

**Question.** What makes two things the same?

**Do.** Sort a tray of buttons by colour. Re-sort the same buttons by size. Ask: are they the same buttons? Yes. Are they in the same groups? No. Why? Because the question changed.

**Words.** same, different, sort, group, because.

**Evidence.** The child can re-sort one set two ways and say which property she used.

**Out of bounds.** Do not say "identical" or "equivalent". Do not ask for a definition of sameness.

## One thing, many names

**Question.** Can one thing have two names?

**Do.** Show a cup, a mug, a beaker. Pass a picture of a dog: *dog*, *puppy*, *hound*, *Rex*. Show a child: *pupil*, *student*, *learner*, *Ana*. Ask: how many things are on the table? How many names?

**Words.** name, thing, word, same thing, different word.

**Evidence.** The child can point to one object and give two names for it, and say that the names are not two objects.

**Out of bounds.** Do not introduce "concept", "category" or "reference". The distinction is practised, not defined.

## True, false, and not sure

**Question.** Is it true, false, or do we not know yet?

**Do.** Three hoops on the floor, labelled *true*, *false*, *not sure*. Read a statement: *There are twenty children in the room. It is raining. The blue box is empty.* Children stand in the hoop that fits. Where the answer depends on looking, they look. Where it cannot be checked, they stand in *not sure* and say what they would need.

**Words.** true, false, not sure, check, find out.

**Evidence.** The child can put a statement in the third hoop without treating it as a failure.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use "probably" as a fourth hoop. Probability belongs to a later band.

## A fact and a guess

**Question.** How do you know?

**Do.** The covered box. Children guess what is inside. Open it. Sort the statements into *I saw it*, *someone told me*, *I guessed*. Repeat with a story: *The fox took the crow's cheese.* Who saw it? Who was told? Who guessed?

**Words.** fact, guess, saw, told, maybe, check.

**Evidence.** The child labels her own statement with one of the three sources.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use the word "evidence" as a mass noun yet. Use "what did you see?" and "who told you?"

## The question and the answer

**Question.** What makes a question a question?

**Do.** Sort a pile of sentences into *asking* and *telling*. Turn a telling into an asking: *The door is closed* → *Is the door closed?* Play twenty questions with a hidden object. The rule: a question must make the answer smaller.

**Words.** question, answer, ask, tell, which, where, who, when.

**Evidence.** The child can turn a statement into a question and ask a question that removes at least one possibility.

**Out of bounds.** Do not teach question words as grammar. The point is the gap, not the punctuation.

## Say it again: the four rules

**Question.** What makes a message work?

**Do.** The teacher gives a deliberately bad instruction. The class fixes it against the four rules: Is it true? Is it enough? Does it fit? Is it clear? Then the class gives instructions to a partner who must follow them exactly.

**Words.** true, enough, fits, clear, again.

**Evidence.** The child can name which of the four rules a broken message broke, with one example.

**Out of bounds.** Do not name Grice, and do not use the word "maxim". The rules are the child's, not the theorist's.

# 2. Year 2 — Things, properties and sources

## ^ The year's question

What is it like, and how do I know?

The second year moves from names to properties and relations, and from statements to sources. The child learns that a fact card has a shape: who or what, what property, where or when, and how we know. The shape is the first fact base, though it is not called that.

## Objects and properties

**Question.** What can a thing be like?

**Do.** A mystery bag. Children put a hand in and describe without naming: *soft, round, cold, heavy*. The class guesses. Then reverse: name the object and list its properties. Sort property cards into *looks*, *feels*, *does*.

**Words.** property, soft, hard, heavy, light, rough, smooth, shape, size.

**Evidence.** The child can describe an object by three properties without naming it, and can name an object from three properties.

**Out of bounds.** Do not introduce "attribute" or "predicate". Property is enough.

## Sort and re-sort

**Question.** Can the same things belong to two different groups?

**Do.** Sort animal cards by habitat, then by diet, then by size. The same card moves. Ask: was the first sort wrong? No. The question changed. Introduce the overlap: a frog is both water and land.

**Words.** group, belong, both, neither, change the question.

**Evidence.** The child can place one object in two groups and explain that the groups answer different questions.

**Out of bounds.** Do not draw Venn diagrams formally. Two hoops on the floor are enough.

## Relations

**Question.** What is between two things?

**Do.** Build a line of children by height. Ask: who is taller than whom? Move one child. What changed? Use family pictures: *Ana is Ben's sister. Ben is Cara's brother. Is Ana Cara's sister?* Use a map: *next to*, *between*, *inside*, *belongs to*.

**Words.** relation, taller, shorter, next to, between, inside, belongs to, sister, brother.

**Evidence.** The child can answer a two-step relation question and show the steps with objects.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use "transitive" or "symmetric". The pattern is shown, not named.

## The fact card

**Question.** What does a fact need?

**Do.** Make a card for a classroom object. Four boxes: *what*, *what is it like*, *where*, *how do I know*. Compare cards. Which box is empty? What happens if the source box is empty? The card is a guess, not a fact.

**Words.** fact card, what, where, how do I know, source.

**Evidence.** The child makes a four-box card with a filled source box.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use a database or a spreadsheet. The card is the technology.

## Where do you know from?

**Question.** Which source is stronger, and why?

**Do.** Three reports of the same event: a child who saw it, a child who heard it from another child, a child who guessed. Rank them. Then introduce the expert: the cook knows what is in the soup; the visitor does not. Ask: who would know best? Why?

**Words.** source, witness, hearsay, expert, guess, stronger, weaker.

**Evidence.** The child ranks three sources and gives one reason for the ranking.

**Out of bounds.** Do not teach "primary" and "secondary" as terms. Use *saw it* and *heard it*.

## Yes, no, and I don't know

**Question.** When is "I don't know" the right answer?

**Do.** A quiz where some questions cannot be answered from the information given. Model the three answers: *yes*, *no*, *I don't know yet*. Ask: what would you need to find out? Add a fourth box: *where would I look?*

**Words.** yes, no, I don't know, find out, look, ask.

**Evidence.** The child chooses "I don't know" when the information is missing and names one way to find out.

**Out of bounds.** Do not treat "I don't know" as a wrong answer. Do not introduce "closed world" as a term; the habit is the point.

# 3. Year 3 — Rules and questions

## ^ The year's question

What follows from what?

The third year is the hinge between the band and the next. The child learns that a rule connects two facts, that a rule can have an exception, and that a question can have a hole. The class builds a paper rule base and explains it aloud. This is the first logic-programming artefact, though it is made of cards.

## If, then

**Question.** What does a rule do?

**Do.** Classroom rules: *If it is raining, we stay inside. If you finish, help someone.* Sort *if* cards and *then* cards into pairs. Test a pair against a story. Which rule fires? Which does not? Introduce the permission form: *If you ride the bike, you must wear a helmet.*

**Words.** if, then, rule, must, may, allowed.

**Evidence.** The child pairs an *if* with a *then* and tests it on two cases.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use arrows or formal notation. Do not use "implication".

## The rule that has an exception

**Question.** What happens when a rule meets a case it did not expect?

**Do.** The rule: *If it is a bird, it can fly.* The penguin. The ostrich. The injured crow. What do we do? Three options: change the rule, add an exception, or say the rule was never a rule. Choose one and say why.

**Words.** rule, exception, unless, except, case, change.

**Evidence.** The child states an exception and says whether it changes the rule or adds to it.

**Out of bounds.** Do not teach "defeasible" or "non-monotonic". The experience is the lesson.

## The hole in the sentence

**Question.** What is missing in *the ___ is on the shelf*?

**Do.** Sentences with a hole. Children fill the hole with a card. Then reverse: given the answer *the red cup*, what was the question? Introduce question cards: *which one?*, *where?*, *who?*. The hole and the question are the same shape.

**Words.** hole, missing, which, where, who, fill, ask.

**Evidence.** The child writes a question with a hole and fills it with two different answers.

**Out of bounds.** Do not use "variable" or "X". The hole is a hole. The word arrives in grades 4–6.

## Ask the wall

**Question.** How do we ask a set of facts a question?

**Do.** Build a fact wall: five cards about the class, each with a source. Ask a question the wall can answer. Ask one it cannot. Mark the second with a *not on the wall* card. The card is not "false". It is "not there".

**Words.** fact wall, ask, answer, on the wall, not on the wall, look.

**Evidence.** The child asks two questions: one the wall answers, one it does not, and marks the second correctly.

**Out of bounds.** Do not say "database" or "knowledge base". The wall is the wall.

## Try one, then the other

**Question.** How do we find all the answers?

**Do.** A box of cards with two possible matches. Pick one, check it, put it back, pick the other. Introduce the two-column search log: *tried*, *result*. Ask: have we tried everything? How do you know? Count the cards.

**Words.** try, check, put back, all, every, next, done.

**Evidence.** The child completes a search log and says whether the search was complete.

**Out of bounds.** Do not teach "backtracking" as a term. Do not use a computer.

## The class rule base

**Question.** Can we make our rules visible?

**Do.** The class writes three rules on cards, each with a name and a source. Add one exception card. Add one *we don't know* card. Test the rule base with three questions. Then each child explains the rule base to an adult, using the four questions: What is on the wall? What follows? What do we ask? What is not on the wall?

**Words.** rule base, fact, rule, question, exception, not on the wall.

**Evidence.** The rule base, and the child's explanation of it. This is the band artefact.

**Out of bounds.** Do not put the rule base on a screen. It stays on paper so that every part can be touched.

# 4. A scripted lesson: the key and the door

**Year 3. Time: 45 minutes. Group: 20–24 children.**

**Question.** If you need a key, is having the key enough?

**Materials.** A cardboard door with a lock drawn on it. Two keys: one that fits, one that does not. A story card with three pictures: Maya with the key, the door locked from the inside, a sign saying *open from inside*. A board divided into *needed* and *enough*.

## ^ Minute 0–5: the door

The door is closed. The teacher holds up the key.

> **Teacher:** The door is locked. I have this key. Can I open the door?

The class says yes. The teacher tries the wrong key. It does not turn.

> **Teacher:** I have a key. Why didn't it open?

The class says: wrong key. The teacher writes *a key* on the board, under *needed*.

> **Teacher:** So a key is needed. Is any key enough?

The class says no. The teacher writes *the right key* under *needed*, and *the right key + the door is not bolted* under *enough*. Do not correct the wording yet. The board is a first draft.

## ^ Minute 5–15: the story

The teacher tells the story with three pictures.

> Maya wants to open the door. She has the right key. She puts it in the lock and turns it. The door does not open. Why not?

Pairs talk for one minute. The teacher takes three answers. The pictures show the answer: the door is bolted from the inside. The sign says *open from inside*.

> **Teacher:** So the right key was needed. Was it enough?

No. Something else was needed: someone inside to unbolt it, or a different way in.

The teacher adds *someone inside* to *needed*, and *the right key + someone inside* to *enough*.

## ^ Minute 15–25: the sort

Each pair gets six cards:

- a key that fits;
- a key that does not fit;
- a door bolted from the inside;
- a door not bolted;
- a person inside;
- nobody inside.

They place the cards under *needed*, *enough*, *both*, *neither*. The teacher circulates and asks one question per pair: **What would you add to make it enough?** The question is the assessment. A pair that can add a missing condition has the concept.

## ^ Minute 25–35: the rules

The class writes two rules on the board.

> If you have the right key and the door is not bolted, you can open the door.
>
> If the door is bolted, the right key is not enough.

The teacher asks: which rule is about *needed*? Which is about *enough*? Which one would a person need to know before trying the key? Why?

## ^ Minute 35–42: say enough

The teacher gives a deliberately incomplete instruction:

> **Teacher:** Go to the cupboard and get the thing.

The class cannot do it. They ask questions: which cupboard? which thing? The teacher answers each question and then asks: **Was my first instruction true? Was it enough?** True, but not enough. The class rewrites it:

> Go to the art cupboard, take the blue tray, and bring it to the front table.

The teacher follows it exactly. The class checks the four rules.

## ^ Minute 42–45: exit ticket

Each child draws two boxes.

> **Box 1:** one thing you need to open a door.
>
> **Box 2:** one thing that is needed but not enough.

The teacher collects the tickets and sorts them into three piles: *needed only*, *needed and enough*, *confused*. The piles are the next lesson's starting point.

## ^ What the teacher watches for

- A child who says "you need the key" after the bolted-door story has the *needed* half. Ask: what else?
- A child who says "you need to be inside" has the *enough* half but has lost the key. Ask: could you open it with no key?
- A child who says "it depends" is ready for the next unit. Ask: depends on what?

## ^ What not to do

Do not introduce the words *necessary* and *sufficient*. The child has the experience. The words arrive in grades 4–6, where they are the unit's vocabulary. Introducing them here produces a label without a handle.

# 5. Assessment

## The three artefacts

**The Question Book.** One page per week. The child writes or draws a question, then marks it: *I can answer this*, *I need to look*, *I need to ask*. The book is not marked for spelling. It is marked for the third column: does the child know what kind of question she has?

**The fact wall.** Five cards. Each has four boxes: *what*, *what is it like*, *where*, *how do I know*. The source box must say *saw*, *told* or *guessed*. A wall with five cards and five sources is the year 2 artefact.

**The rule base.** Three rules, one exception, one *we don't know* card. The child explains it aloud to an adult for two minutes. The adult asks the four questions: What is on the wall? What follows? What do we ask? What is not on the wall? The child's answers are the year 3 artefact.

## The gate descriptors

| Gate | The child can... | The evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | name a thing and give a second name for it | points to one object, gives two names, says they are one thing |
| 2 | sort one set two ways and say why the groups changed | re-sorts the same buttons and explains the new property |
| 2 | ask a question with a hole and fill it two ways | writes *which ___?* and gives two answers |
| 3 | mark a statement true, false or not sure, and say what would decide it | uses the third hoop without prompting; names a check |
| 3 | say where a fact came from | labels her own statement *saw*, *told* or *guessed* |
| 4 (seed) | follow a rule with an exception and explain the exception | tests the bird rule on a penguin and says what changed |

## Moderation

Two teachers, one artefact, blind. Each writes one sentence: what the child can do, and what she cannot yet do. If the sentences disagree, the artefact is ambiguous. Fix the task, not the child.

## What not to assess

Do not assess the speed of sorting. Do not assess handwriting. Do not assess the recall of the four rules by name. A child who uses the rules and cannot name them has the habit. A child who names them and does not use them has a poster.

# 6. Common errors, and what to do

| What the child says | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| "It's called a dog, so it is a dog." | The name has replaced the thing | Show two names for one object; ask what is on the floor |
| "It's true because I like it." | Truth confused with preference | Use a true statement the child dislikes; sort it together |
| "I guessed, so I lied." | Guess confused with lie | Separate *not knowing* from *intending to deceive* |
| "A rule is always true." | No experience of exceptions | Give the bird rule a penguin; ask what to change |
| "I don't know" (as a full stop) | The third answer has no next step | Add *where would you look?* to the third hoop |
| "I said it, so you should understand." | The listener is invisible | Play the exact-instruction game; follow only the words |
| "More words means better." | Quantity confused with quality | The listener acts; stop when the action is possible |

## The one error to pre-empt

**The name is the thing.** It is the error the whole band exists to prevent. A child who thinks the word *dog* is a dog will later think the word *valid* is an argument, the word *average* is a person, and the word *safe* is a guarantee. The correction is simple and must be repeated: point to the thing, then to the name, and ask which one can be patted.

# 7. Out of bounds

The following are out of bounds for the whole band. They are not forbidden forever. They are forbidden here because they would produce a word without a habit.

| Not this | Why not yet | Where it enters |
|---|---|---|
| Formal notation (`→`, `∧`, `∀`) | The symbol would replace the sentence | Grades 7–9 |
| Truth tables | The table would replace the picture | Grades 10–12 |
| Named fallacies | The name would replace the counterexample | Grades 7–9 |
| "Valid", "premise", "conclusion" as technical terms | The words would be learned before the objects | Grades 7–9 |
| "Implicature" as a term | The question is teachable; the label is not yet useful | Grades 7–9 |
| Prolog or any programming environment | The paper rule base must be secure first | Grades 4–6, supervised |
| Base rates and probability | The child has no population to reason about | Grades 10–12 |
| The word "logic" as a subject name | It turns a practice into a timetable label | Grades 4–6 |

## The screen rule

There is no independent screen use in this band. A teacher may show a picture on a screen. A child may not use a program. The reason is not that screens are bad. It is that the band's central object — the fact card and the paper rule — must be touchable before it becomes virtual.

# 8. What a school needs to provide

## Space and time

One 40-minute lesson per week, in a room with a floor for sorting and a wall for the fact cards. The wall must stay up between lessons. A fact wall that is put away is a worksheet.

## Materials

- 200 blank cards, A6, in five colours.
- Three floor hoops.
- A covered box.
- A set of object cards: animals, household objects, people.
- One cardboard door and two keys, for the scripted lesson.
- A large sheet of paper for the class rule base.

The materials cost less than a class set of textbooks and last three years.

## People

One teacher per class, with one half-day of training on the five-move lesson and the out-of-bounds list. A teaching assistant can run the sorting and the search log. The class teacher must run the naming and the explanation, because those are the parts that require judgement.

## What success looks like

A visitor asks a child, "How do you know?" The child answers with one of three sources, not with a shrug. A visitor asks, "What does this rule not cover?" The child gives an exception. A visitor asks, "What is on the wall?" The child points to the fact cards and then to the *not on the wall* card. That is the band, in three answers.
